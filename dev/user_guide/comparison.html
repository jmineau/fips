Choosing a method#

fips solves a problem whose state cannot be negative (a flux, a concentration, a rate) in four ways: the analytic posterior, which ignores the bound, "bounded" with lower=0, and "gauss_newton" with a LognormalForward or a SoftplusForward. This page runs the four on one example, a problem shaped like a Salt Lake Valley methane inversion (the flux of each cell of a grid, with an inventory as its prior, seen from a few sites), with one prior written four ways, and scores each on many sets of synthetic data (Synthetic experiments). What the example’s numbers say, in short:

  • for a total of the whole state (here, a basin’s), the analytic posterior is good enough: its total and its interval held in every world tried here, at a hundredth of the others’ cost or less;

  • for a map, the lognormal model is best, and its intervals are right when the flux is a factor of the inventory, correlated as its prior says;

  • bounds change the prior: a Gaussian truncated at zero has a higher mean than the Gaussian, so the draws of "bounded" put the total far above an inventory that is the mean. Its mode does not;

  • the softplus model is right in its own world, and about 5% low on the total in a lognormal one.

The rest of the page is the experiment behind them, small enough to run here, then the same experiment at the Salt Lake Valley’s size.

The problem#

A grid of cells, two sites, and observations each of which sees the cells upwind of its site, in that hour’s wind direction:

In [1]: import time

In [2]: import numpy as np

In [3]: import pandas as pd

In [4]: import fips

In [5]: side, n_obs, n_sites = 10, 40, 2  # cells on a side, observations, sites

In [6]: trials, n_draws = 40, 100  # trials of each world, draws in each trial

In [7]: rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

In [8]: x, y = np.meshgrid(np.arange(side), np.arange(side))

In [9]: xy = np.c_[x.ravel(), y.ravel()].astype(float)

In [10]: state = fips.Space({"flux": pd.MultiIndex.from_arrays([xy[:, 0], xy[:, 1]], names=["x", "y"])})

In [11]: observed = fips.Space({"ch4": pd.RangeIndex(n_obs, name="obs_id")})

In [12]: sites = rng.uniform(0, side - 1, (n_sites, 2))

In [13]: at = rng.integers(0, n_sites, n_obs)  # the site of each observation

In [14]: footprint = np.zeros((n_obs, side * side))

In [15]: for i in range(n_obs):
   ....:     wind = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi)
   ....:     along, across = ((xy - sites[at[i]]) @ [[np.cos(wind), -np.sin(wind)], [np.sin(wind), np.cos(wind)]]).T
   ....:     footprint[i] = np.exp(-np.abs(across) - np.maximum(along, 0.0) / 3.0) * (along > -1.0)
   ....: 

In [16]: footprint[footprint < 0.02] = 0.0

In [17]: footprint *= 5.0 / footprint.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

In [18]: H = fips.Operator(footprint, observed, state)

In [19]: inventory = fips.Vector(rng.lognormal(0.0, 0.8, side * side), state)

One prior, written four ways. Each cell’s prior flux has the inventory as its mean and 131% of it as its standard deviation (the spread of a lognormal of log standard deviation 1, the setting of the Salt Lake Valley-shaped tests in Fluxes that stay positive), and the errors are correlated over two cells. The analytic and bounded methods write it as a Gaussian of the flux; prior_for gives each transform the prior on \(u\) whose flux has the same mean and standard deviation, and the correlation is then that of \(u\). The softplus model bends at the median inventory:

In [20]: std = np.sqrt(np.e - 1.0) * inventory

In [21]: lognormal = fips.LognormalForward(H, inventory.to_series("flux"))

In [22]: softplus = fips.SoftplusForward(H, scale=float(np.median(inventory.values)))

In [23]: priors = {
   ....:     "analytic": (inventory, std, H),
   ....:     "bounded": (inventory, std, H),
   ....:     "lognormal": (*lognormal.prior_for(inventory, std), lognormal),
   ....:     "softplus": (*softplus.prior_for(inventory, std), softplus),
   ....: }
   ....: 

In [24]: def build(method, obs):
   ....:     """The problem of the observations `obs`, as `method` writes it."""
   ....:     mean, sd, forward = priors[method]
   ....:     return fips.Problem(
   ....:         prior=mean,
   ....:         prior_cov=fips.Exponential(["x", "y"], 2.0).with_std(sd),
   ....:         obs=obs,
   ....:         obs_cov=fips.Independent().with_std(0.3),
   ....:         forward=forward,
   ....:         offset=1900.0,
   ....:     )
   ....: 

In [25]: linear = build("analytic", fips.Vector(np.zeros(n_obs), observed))

In [26]: start = linear.predict(inventory)  # the data the inventory gives

Most cells are seen weakly, as in a valley with a few sites: about half have an averaging kernel below 0.1.

In [27]: seen = fips.solve(build("analytic", start)).dofs_per_element.values

In [28]: round(float(np.mean(seen < 0.1)), 2)
Out[28]: 0.51

Each method’s solve, and its flux (a transform’s estimate and draws are \(u\), and transform maps them to the flux). Each trial’s posterior is post.with_data(obs); a "gauss_newton" one makes 50 draws to tune its sampler (burn_in) before those asked of it:

In [29]: def solve(method, obs):
   ....:     """The method's posterior of the observations `obs`."""
   ....:     if method == "analytic":
   ....:         return fips.solve(build(method, obs))
   ....:     if method == "bounded":
   ....:         return fips.solve(build(method, obs), "bounded", lower=0.0)
   ....:     return fips.solve(build(method, obs), "gauss_newton", burn_in=50)
   ....: 

In [30]: def flux(method, x):
   ....:     """The flux of a state, or of draws: the transform's, or the state itself."""
   ....:     return priors[method][2].transform(x) if method in ("lognormal", "softplus") else x
   ....:

Two worlds#

A lognormal truth. Each cell’s flux is the inventory times a lognormal factor whose mean is 1, so the inventory is the mean flux, and the logarithms of the factors correlate over two cells, as the priors do. The truth’s mean, standard deviation and correlations are the priors’; its shape is the lognormal model’s and none of the others’. This is the world of an inventory that is right on average and wrong by a factor in each place:

In [31]: field = fips.Exponential(["x", "y"], 2.0).with_std(1.0).build(state)

In [32]: def lognormal_world(rng):
   ....:     """A truth lognormal about the inventory (its mean), and the observations it gives."""
   ....:     truth = inventory * np.exp(field.sample(rng) - 0.5)
   ....:     return truth, linear.predict(truth) + linear.obs_cov.sample(rng)
   ....:

Each method’s own world. The truth is a draw from the method’s own prior and the data come through its own model, so an exact posterior’s intervals hold the truth as often as they claim. This checks that each method is right on its own terms. The lognormal model’s own world is the lognormal truth above. The bounded method’s prior is the Gaussian truncated at zero, which is the bounded posterior of a problem with no data, so its truths are that posterior’s draws:

In [33]: def own_world(method):
   ....:     """Truths from the method's prior and data through its model, the truth as a flux."""
   ....:     problem = build(method, start)
   ....:     def world(rng):
   ....:         truth, obs = problem.simulate(rng=rng)
   ....:         return flux(method, truth), obs
   ....:     return world
   ....: 

In [34]: nothing = fips.Space({"ch4": pd.RangeIndex(1, name="obs_id")})

In [35]: no_data = fips.Problem(inventory, fips.Exponential(["x", "y"], 2.0).with_std(std), fips.Vector([0.0], nothing), fips.Independent().with_std(1.0), fips.Operator(np.zeros((1, side * side)), nothing, state))

In [36]: truncated = fips.solve(no_data, "bounded", lower=0.0).sample(trials, rng=100)  # one truth a column

In [37]: def truncated_world(rng, truths=iter(truncated.T.to_numpy())):
   ....:     """The bounded method's own world: the truncated prior's draws, one a trial."""
   ....:     truth = fips.Vector(next(truths), state)
   ....:     return truth, linear.predict(truth) + linear.obs_cov.sample(rng)
   ....:

Scoring#

Each trial, the world makes a truth and its data, the method solves them, and the trial’s posterior is compared with the truth, in flux: the total of all cells and each cell. Every method’s posterior is read the same way, from n_draws draws of it (sample()):

  • the total: its estimate is the mean of the draws’ totals, its interval the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of the draws’ totals;

  • each cell: its interval is the quantiles of its draws, and its error is that of the estimate (the mode, or the analytic mean), as a root mean square over the cells divided by that of the inventory: 1 is no better than the prior.

This is trials()’ loop, written out, because trials compares totals of the state, and a transform’s state is \(u\): a total of the flux is not a linear function of \(u\). Its intervals are read the same way: trials’ lower and upper are the quantiles of each trial’s draws for a sampled posterior ("bounded", a transform) and the estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard deviations for the analytic one, which is that posterior’s interval. Every world draws from one generator and every method’s draws from another, so the four methods get the same data, trial for trial:

In [38]: def score(method, world, seed=1):
   ....:     """Each trial's error and whether its 95% intervals (the draws' quantiles) hold the truth, in flux."""
   ....:     post, rows, clock = solve(method, start), [], time.perf_counter()
   ....:     g_world, g_draws = np.random.default_rng(seed), np.random.default_rng(seed + 1)
   ....:     for _ in range(trials):
   ....:         truth, obs = world(g_world)
   ....:         trial = post.with_data(obs)
   ....:         t, draws = truth.values, flux(method, trial.sample(n_draws, rng=g_draws)).to_numpy()
   ....:         low, high = np.quantile(draws, [0.025, 0.975], axis=1)
   ....:         totals, error = draws.sum(axis=0), flux(method, trial.estimate).values - t
   ....:         rows.append(
   ....:             {
   ....:                 "total error": totals.mean() - t.sum(),
   ....:                 "total std": totals.std(),
   ....:                 "total covered": np.quantile(totals, 0.025) <= t.sum() <= np.quantile(totals, 0.975),
   ....:                 "cells covered": np.mean((low <= t) & (t <= high)),
   ....:                 "cell error": np.sqrt(np.mean(error**2) / np.mean((inventory.values - t) ** 2)),
   ....:             }
   ....:         )
   ....:     return pd.DataFrame(rows).assign(seconds=(time.perf_counter() - clock) / trials)
   ....:

The summary has the total’s bias, its standard error, the spread of the total’s error over the trials and the spread the posterior claims (each as a fraction of the inventory’s total, as every bias on this page is), and how often the total’s interval holds the truth; then the cells’ coverage with its standard error, the cell error, and the seconds a trial takes:

In [39]: def summarize(table):
   ....:     """The total's bias, spread and coverage, the cells' coverage and error, with standard errors."""
   ....:     n, e = len(table), table["total error"] / inventory.values.sum()
   ....:     return pd.Series(
   ....:         {
   ....:             "bias": e.mean(),
   ....:             "bias se": e.std() / np.sqrt(n),
   ....:             "spread": e.std(),
   ....:             "claimed": table["total std"].mean() / inventory.values.sum(),
   ....:             "coverage": table["total covered"].mean(),
   ....:             "cells": table["cells covered"].mean(),
   ....:             "cells se": table["cells covered"].std() / np.sqrt(n),
   ....:             "cell error": table["cell error"].mean(),
   ....:             "s a trial": table["seconds"].iloc[0],
   ....:         }
   ....:     )
   ....:

What the numbers mean#

Every method is right on its own terms. In its own world each one’s intervals hold the truth about as often as they claim, for the total and the cells, and its total is unbiased. The differences come from what each prior says when the world is not its own.

The analytic posterior is good enough for a basin total. In the lognormal truth its total is unbiased (+0.7%, with a standard error of 1.0%) and its interval holds the truth 97% of the time; its cells’ intervals cover 95.5%. The Gaussian posterior puts some cells below zero, but a total sums many cells, and here the sum of their errors is close enough to Gaussian. It costs a few milliseconds a solve.

The lognormal model makes the best map. Its cell error is 6% lower than the analytic posterior’s in the lognormal truth (0.714 against 0.763) and 4% lower with independent cells, and its total is as good as the analytic one, with an interval a third narrower (0.115 against 0.165) that still covers. Its cells’ intervals are right when the flux is a factor of the inventory with the correlations its prior assumes, and too narrow (0.86) when the truth’s factors are less correlated than its prior says. A cell with no inventory stays at zero (Fluxes that stay positive).

Bounds change the prior, and the change reaches the total. The bounded method’s prior is the Gaussian prior truncated at zero, and truncation raises the mean. A Gaussian truncated at zero never has a standard deviation above its mean, so none has the inventory as its mean and 131% of it as its standard deviation; and cells that are correlated and must all be positive are pushed up together. In this problem the truncated prior’s mean total is 2.36 times the inventory’s (the mean of the truths of the bounded method’s own world; a Gibbs sampler, independent of fips, gives 2.10 against fips’s 2.12 for a 36-cell version). Where the data barely see a cell, its posterior is that prior, so the draws put the total above the truth by 56% of the inventory’s total when the inventory is the mean, and their interval almost never holds it. With a standard deviation of 50% of the inventory instead of 131%, the draws’ total was still 9% high (400 cells, 100 trials, a standard error of 0.6%) and its interval held the truth in 57% of the trials. The mode is not moved: a weakly seen cell’s mode stays at its inventory, so the sum of the mode is within 2.5% of the truth in the lognormal worlds, and the mode’s map is about the analytic one (cell error 0.750 against 0.763). But the mode’s total is not the bounded posterior’s: in the bounded method’s own world, whose truths are the truncated prior’s, it is below the truth by 45% of the inventory’s total, and an interval of the mode’s total plus or minus 1.96 standard deviations held the truth in 2.5% of the trials. So use "bounded" for a map that must not go below zero, and do not read a total, or a cell’s interval, from its draws unless the truncated prior is the prior you mean. These tables are of the truncated draws; the rectified draws (Rectified draws) shift a total less, and were not run here.

The softplus model is a Gaussian prior where the flux is large and bends near zero. Its own world is right; in the lognormal truth its total is 4.6% low (five standard errors), its cells’ intervals are a little too wide (0.976), and its map is about the analytic posterior’s.

Which interval to read. Every interval here is a quantile of the draws. For the analytic posterior that is the estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard deviations; for a sampled one, "bounded" or a transform, the quantiles of its draws, which is how uncertainty() and trials() give its lower and upper. Read as the mode plus or minus 1.96 standard deviations instead, the bounded method’s cells covered 0.90 in its own world (0.945 from quantiles), and its total 0.025 (0.955). A total of a transform’s flux is not a total of its state: take it from sample().

These numbers are for one problem: a prior of 131% correlated over two cells, these footprints and this noise. Another inversion’s own trials, at its own size, say how its methods compare; this page is the code to run them.

What each costs#

At 400 cells, on one core: an analytic solve took 0.014 s, a bounded one 0.016 s, and a "gauss_newton" one, which finds the mode, 0.05 to 0.06 s (a median of 7 Newton or Gauss-Newton steps, 9 for the softplus model in its own world). The spread is where they differ: the default 2000 draws took 6 s for "bounded", 17 s for the lognormal model and 23 s for the softplus model, against no draws for the analytic posterior. A trial with 400 draws took 0.013 s for the analytic posterior, 0.2 to 1.7 s for "bounded" (more where more cells sit near the bound) and 4.4 to 5.2 s for either transform: 200 trials of a transform take about 17 minutes. The s a trial column above measures the same at the page’s size.
