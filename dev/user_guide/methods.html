Choosing a method#

fips.solve(problem, method, **options) solves one Problem by one of five methods. The problem is the same for all of them: a prior and its covariance, observations and theirs, and a forward map. The methods differ in what they allow (bounds, a forward model that is not linear, a model that can only be run, a problem too large to factor) and so in what their answer is. Start with "analytic", the default: it is exact, it gives every diagnostic, and every other method gives its answer when the problem is linear and nothing else is asked of it.

Which one#

Ask in this order:

  1. Can the forward map be written as a matrix, and does the problem fit in memory? Use "analytic". Most problems stop here, and a large sparse forward operator with a Kronecker prior covariance still fits.

  2. Must part of the state stay at or above a bound (a quantity that cannot be negative)? Use "bounded", or solve for a transform of the state that is always positive (LogNormalForward, SoftplusForward) with "gauss_newton". The two change the prior in different ways: Keeping a quantity positive below.

  3. Is the forward map a model that is not linear, with a Jacobian you can write? Use "gauss_newton", after checking the Jacobian with fips.taylortest().

  4. Can the model only be run (no matrix, no Jacobian)? Use "ensemble": draw members, run the model on each, and update them.

  5. Is the problem too large to factor, or is the model given by a tangent linear and an adjoint? Use "variational".

Two questions apply to every method: whether the covariance hyperparameters (the prior’s standard deviation and correlation length, the observation error) are right, which Fitting the covariance hyperparameters answers from the data, and whether their uncertainty matters to the answer, which The uncertainty of the hyperparameters carries into it.

Three kinds of answer#

Every method returns a Posterior, but not every posterior can answer every question. There are three kinds:

  • a Gaussian posterior: the estimate is the mean and the covariance is known ("analytic", "ensemble", and "variational" when its rank holds every direction the data inform);

  • a sampled posterior: the estimate is the mode, and the mean, the variances and the intervals come from draws the solve keeps ("bounded", "gauss_newton");

  • a low-rank posterior: the covariance is the prior’s less the rank directions the observations inform most ("variational").

Which interval you get follows from the kind. For a Gaussian or low-rank posterior, the lower and upper of uncertainty() and trials() are the estimate plus or minus z standard deviations. For a sampled one they are quantiles of the draws, which follow the posterior’s skew. The reduction columns compare against the prior the method used, so observations that tell nothing reduce nothing: for "bounded" the prior with the bounds (cut at them, or set to them for rectified draws), from draws of it made as the posterior’s are; for "ensemble" the spread of its prior members; for the others the problem’s prior. A member a kind cannot give raises UnavailableError; for example, the data worth needs an estimate linear in the observations, which only the analytic method gives. The members table lists every member for every kind.

Keeping a quantity positive#

A quantity that cannot be negative has three answers in fips, and they are not the same answer: each puts a different prior on the state.

  • The analytic posterior ignores the bound. Some elements may come out negative where the observations see little, but an aggregate of many elements is often close to Gaussian, and its estimate and interval can be trusted. It is also by far the cheapest.

  • "bounded" with lower=0 keeps the Gaussian prior and cuts it at the bound. Its mode is the analytic estimate moved onto the bound along the posterior’s correlations. Its draws are of the cut posterior, whose mean is higher than the Gaussian’s wherever the data are weak, so an aggregate read from its draws sits above a prior mean you meant as the mean. Use it for an element-by-element estimate that must not cross the bound; read an aggregate from its draws only if the cut prior is the prior you mean. Rectified draws shift an aggregate less.

  • LogNormalForward solves for the logarithm of a factor of a reference value, so the uncertainty is relative (“a factor of 2”), and an element whose reference is zero stays zero. SoftplusForward solves for a value that is itself where it is large and bends to stay positive near zero, so the uncertainty is additive (“10 ± 2”). Both keep the prior’s mean and standard deviation as you give them (prior_for), and draw the posterior exactly.

Comparing ways to keep a quantity positive runs all four on one synthetic problem, in worlds that match each prior and in one that matches only the lognormal. Its conclusions, for that problem: every method’s intervals hold the truth about as often as they claim in its own world; the analytic posterior’s aggregate is unbiased wherever the truth is correlated as the priors say, and its interval covers in every world tried; the lognormal transform gives the best element estimates when the truth is a factor of the reference; and the bounded method’s draws put an aggregate well above a truth whose mean is the prior’s. Run the same comparison on your own problem with Synthetic experiments.

Reading a transformed state#

With LogNormalForward or SoftplusForward, the state is the variable \(u\), not the value. model.transform maps the estimate, or each draw, to the value. An aggregate of the value comes from the draws: transform the draws, then aggregate. post.sample(n, W=W) aggregates \(u\), which is not an aggregate of the value. The same holds for trials(), which aggregates the state: write the loop over draws yourself, as Comparing ways to keep a quantity positive does.

Warnings#

Each method warns when something it found changes how far its answer can be trusted (a prior wide against its bounds, draws that mix slowly, steps that converge slowly, rank too small to hold what the data inform). Each warning is a FipsWarning, which Python shows without any setup, once for each line of your code that leads to it; make them errors or hide them with warnings.filterwarnings(). fips also logs each one under the fips logger (Repeated inversions, logging and saving).

Cost#

The analytic method factors one matrix once; a few thousand state elements or observations take a fraction of a second, and a gamma sweep or new data reuse the factors. "bounded" finds its mode for little more, but its draws, made the first time a spread is asked for, cost a step of the state’s size for each time a draw meets a bound. "gauss_newton" costs a solve per iteration (typically a handful) and then its draws, each several runs of the model and its Jacobian’s transpose. "ensemble" costs one run of the model per member, done by you. "variational" costs products with \(H\) and its transpose: one per conjugate-gradient step, and rank more for the uncertainty. Each method’s page says what it scales with.
