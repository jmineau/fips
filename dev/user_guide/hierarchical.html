The uncertainty of the settings#

A posterior at fitted settings (Fitting the covariance settings) treats them as known. Give each setting a prior, and fips.hierarchical() draws the settings from their posterior and the state at each of those draws, so the state’s intervals hold the uncertainty of the settings too. The call takes the function that builds the problem from the settings, and a prior for each setting by name:

result = fips.hierarchical(build, {"std": LogNormal(np.log(0.5), 1.0), ...})

What comes back is a Hierarchical: the kept draws of the settings, and one draw of the state at each. Its estimate is the mean of the state’s draws, and its spread and intervals are theirs. It is not a Posterior: the posterior of the state is a mixture of Gaussians, one for each draw of the settings, and what is defined only for one Gaussian comes from the posterior at one setting, result.at(settings).

When to use it#

  • Few observations decide the settings, and the answer depends on them. The settings’ uncertainty then widens the state’s intervals. With many observations it widens them little, and a fit (Fitting the covariance settings) is enough; result.at(result.mode) shows the difference on your own problem.

  • The only settings are a factor on the prior standard deviation and one on the observation standard deviation. Pass the problem itself and take The fast path, which costs about one eigendecomposition for the whole run.

  • For one best value of the settings, maximize the evidence instead (Fitting the covariance settings): it is cheaper, and the posterior at the fit has every diagnostic.

  • Not for a problem the analytic method cannot solve (a forward model, bounds, a problem too large to factor): each step needs the exact evidence and exact draws of the analytic posterior.

An example#

The example of Fitting the covariance settings: 150 state elements along a line, one unit apart, 200 observations of one to four neighboring elements, and observations drawn from a world at a prior standard deviation of 1, a correlation length of 5 and an observation standard deviation of 0.5.

In [1]: import numpy as np

In [2]: import pandas as pd

In [3]: import fips

In [4]: from fips.distributions import LogNormal

In [5]: rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

In [6]: n, m = 150, 200

In [7]: line = fips.Space({"field": pd.Index(np.arange(n) + 0.5, name="x")})

In [8]: observed = fips.Space({"obs": pd.RangeIndex(m, name="obs_id")})

In [9]: weights = np.zeros((m, n))

In [10]: for i, start in enumerate(rng.integers(0, n - 4, m)):
   ....:     weights[i, start : start + rng.integers(1, 5)] = rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5)
   ....: 

In [11]: H = fips.Operator(weights, observed, line)

In [12]: prior = fips.Vector(np.full(n, 2.0), line, name="prior")

The function takes the settings as a dict, by the names their priors have:

In [13]: def build(settings, obs, H=H):
   ....:     """Return the problem at the settings (prior std, correlation length, observation std)."""
   ....:     return fips.Problem(
   ....:         prior=prior,
   ....:         prior_cov=fips.Exponential("x", settings["length"]).with_std(settings["std"]),
   ....:         obs=obs,
   ....:         obs_cov=fips.Independent().with_std(settings["noise"]),
   ....:         forward=H,
   ....:     )
   ....: 

In [14]: true = {"std": 1.0, "length": 5.0, "noise": 0.5}

In [15]: truth, obs = build(true, fips.Vector(np.zeros(m), observed)).simulate(rng)

Each setting’s prior is a LogNormal that knows it to a factor of \(e\) (a standard deviation of 1 in its log), centered a factor of two or more from the truth:

In [16]: priors = {
   ....:     "std": LogNormal(np.log(0.5), 1.0),
   ....:     "length": LogNormal(np.log(2.0), 1.0),
   ....:     "noise": LogNormal(np.log(1.0), 1.0),
   ....: }
   ....: 

In [17]: result = fips.hierarchical(lambda s: build(s, obs), priors, draws=1000, burn_in=300, seed=0)

In [18]: result.settings.quantile([0.025, 0.5, 0.975]).T.assign(true=pd.Series(true), mode=result.mode).round(3)
Out[18]: 
         0.025    0.5  0.975  true   mode
setting                                  
std      0.784  0.982  1.214   1.0  0.965
length   1.741  2.793  5.485   5.0  2.751
noise    0.418  0.481  0.564   0.5  0.477

The two standard deviations are known to within about a fifth, and each 95% interval holds its truth. The correlation length is known only to a factor of about three, as the profile of Fitting the covariance settings found.

Reading the result#

The settings. settings is the kept draws, one row per draw and one column per setting; mode is the posterior mode in the coordinates the sampler works in (the log of a positive setting, the logit of a bounded one), mapped back: close to the fit of Fitting the covariance settings, but not the peak of the settings’ density itself. Two numbers say whether the chain can be trusted: acceptance, the fraction of proposals accepted (near 0.3 when the proposal is tuned), and ess, each setting’s effective sample size: about as many independent draws as the correlated ones are worth.

In [19]: round(result.acceptance, 2), result.ess.round().to_dict()
Out[19]: (0.31, {'std': 79.0, 'length': 61.0, 'noise': 102.0})

A setting’s mean is known to its standard deviation over the root of its effective sample size. For a smaller error, ask for more draws.

The state. estimate, variances, cov(), uncertainty() and sample() read the state, or its aggregates with W, from the draws, as a Posterior’s members of the same names do. The intervals are the draws’ quantiles, and uncertainty has no prior columns: the prior of the state depends on the settings.

One setting. at() gives the ordinary posterior at one value of every setting: the gain, DOFS, the chi-squared tests, the evidence. At the mode, its intervals are those of a fit, which leave the settings’ uncertainty out. Here, the sum over each third of the line, with and without the settings’ uncertainty:

In [20]: x = line.select("field")

In [21]: parts = pd.Series(np.select([x < 50, x < 100], ["left", "middle"], "right"), index=x, name="part")

In [22]: W = fips.aggregate.sum(line, by=parts)

In [23]: def compare(result, truth, level=0.9):
   ....:     """Each aggregate's truth, and its interval with the settings' uncertainty and at their mode."""
   ....:     columns = ["posterior_std", "lower", "upper"]
   ....:     with_settings = result.uncertainty(W, level=level)[columns]
   ....:     at_mode = result.at(result.mode).uncertainty(W, level=level)[columns]
   ....:     true_value = pd.DataFrame({"": (W @ truth).values}, index=with_settings.index)
   ....:     table = pd.concat({"with settings": with_settings, "at mode": at_mode, "truth": true_value}, axis=1)
   ....:     return table.droplevel("block")
   ....: 

In [24]: compare(result, truth).round(2)
Out[24]: 
       with settings                       at mode                   truth
       posterior_std   lower   upper posterior_std   lower   upper        
part                                                                      
left            1.61  110.45  115.71          1.58  110.66  115.87  112.52
middle          1.97   98.15  104.46          1.95   98.15  104.55  101.74
right           1.96  106.69  113.08          1.95  106.80  113.20  109.10

The two agree closely: two hundred observations decide these aggregates, and the correlation length, the setting known least, moves how an aggregate splits among its elements more than the aggregate itself.

The priors of the settings#

A setting’s prior is one of the three distributions of fips.distributions:

  • LogNormal (mu, sigma): a positive setting known to a factor, with median \(e^{\mu}\). The usual choice for a standard deviation or a correlation length.

  • HalfNormal (scale): a positive setting that may be near zero.

  • Uniform (low, high): a setting known only to lie in an interval.

The search for the settings’ mode starts at the priors’ medians. A prior on a setting is a claim like any other, and the intervals are only as good as it. A prior centered far from the truth pulls the settings toward it, and with few observations the data do not undo all of the pull.

The fast path#

When the only settings are a factor on the prior standard deviation and one on the observation standard deviation, pass the problem built at factors of 1 instead of a function, and name the settings:

  • "prior_scale": the prior standard deviation is multiplied by it;

  • "obs_scale": the observation standard deviation is multiplied by it; or "gamma" in its place: the observation covariance is divided by it.

One eigendecomposition then gives the evidence at every step without a solve, and the state’s draws at every kept step in one product, whatever the size of the problem.

Fast or not, the settings’ uncertainty matters most when few observations decide them. Fifteen observations, all in the left two thirds of the line, with the correlation length held at 5:

In [25]: few = fips.Space({"obs": pd.RangeIndex(15, name="obs_id")})

In [26]: sparse = np.zeros((15, n))

In [27]: for i, start in enumerate(rng.integers(0, 96, 15)):
   ....:     sparse[i, start : start + rng.integers(1, 5)] = rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5)
   ....: 

In [28]: H_few = fips.Operator(sparse, few, line)

In [29]: truth_few, obs_few = build(true, fips.Vector(np.zeros(15), few), H=H_few).simulate(rng)

In [30]: unit = build({"std": 1.0, "length": 5.0, "noise": 1.0}, obs_few, H=H_few)

In [31]: scales = {"prior_scale": LogNormal(np.log(0.5), 1.0), "obs_scale": LogNormal(0.0, 1.0)}

In [32]: fast = fips.hierarchical(unit, scales, seed=0)

In [33]: fast.settings.quantile([0.025, 0.5, 0.975]).round(3)
Out[33]: 
setting  prior_scale  obs_scale
0.025          0.556      0.239
0.500          0.897      0.563
0.975          1.575      1.468

In [34]: compare(fast, truth_few).round(2)
Out[34]: 
       with settings                      at mode                  truth
       posterior_std  lower   upper posterior_std  lower   upper        
part                                                                    
left           13.27  63.47  106.72         12.16  63.54  103.54   74.70
middle         10.85  97.83  132.99         10.36  98.15  132.23  108.50
right          20.48  68.73  135.50         19.68  69.35  134.08   81.72

Here each standard deviation is known only to a factor of several, and the intervals with the settings’ uncertainty are wider than those at the mode. Over many such worlds whose settings are drawn from their priors, the intervals with the settings’ uncertainty hold the truth about as often as they claim, and those at the mode less often.

Options#

Option

Default

What it does

build

(required)

The function from a dict of settings to the Problem, or to its analytic posterior (fips.solve(problem, gamma=s["gamma"]) makes gamma a setting). Or the problem itself, for the fast path.

settings

(required)

The prior of each setting, by the name build reads it by. Their order is the order of the columns of result.settings.

draws

2000

The kept steps of the chain, each with one draw of the state.

burn_in

500

The steps before them, which tune the proposal and are not kept.

seed

0

The seed of the chain and of the state’s draws (an integer), so a run is reproducible. The two have their own streams: the chain is the same whatever the state’s draws are.

Warnings and checks#

  • The log posterior does not curve down in some direction at the mode (a warning): the data and the prior hardly decide a setting, or a combination of them. The proposal takes a variance of 1 there and the burn-in scales it, but the chain may be slow: look at the effective sample sizes, and give that setting a narrower prior or hold it fixed.

  • A low acceptance or a small effective sample size: run more draws or a longer burn_in. An INFO record gives both at the end of each run, and another says when the search for the mode stopped early.

  • A problem that does not solve at some settings (a covariance that is not positive definite) has no density there, and the chain does not step to it. At the priors’ medians it raises a ValueError, because the search for the mode starts there.

  • A function that returns a posterior of another method raises a TypeError: return the problem, or its analytic posterior. A prior that is not one of fips.distributions raises a TypeError.

  • The fast path takes only its three names, and only one of "obs_scale" and "gamma"; any other raises a ValueError. For other settings, pass a function.

Cost#

A step of the chain is one solve of the problem the function builds, so a run costs burn_in + draws solves, plus a few dozen for the search for the mode and its Hessian (more with more settings). A step of the fast path costs a sum over the smaller of the number of observations and the number of state elements, after one eigendecomposition. The draws of the state are kept as a state x draws array: 2000 draws of 30,000 elements take 480 MB, so for a large state ask for fewer draws, or keep the aggregates you need.

How it works#

With a prior \(p(s)\) on the settings, their posterior is the prior times the evidence of the problem built at them (the evidence, exact for a linear problem):

\[p(s \mid z) \propto p(s) \, p(z \mid s).\]

The posterior of the state is the posterior at each setting, averaged over the settings’ posterior,

\[p(x \mid z) = \int p(x \mid s, z) \, p(s \mid z) \, ds,\]

so a draw of the settings from \(p(s \mid z)\), then of the state from \(p(x \mid s, z)\) (exactly, as sample() draws it), is a draw of \(p(x \mid z)\). Its variance is

\[\operatorname{var}(x \mid z) = E\big[\hat{S}(s)\big] + \operatorname{var}\big[\hat{x}(s)\big]\]

over the settings’ posterior: the average of each setting’s posterior variance, and the spread of the estimates between settings. A solve at fitted settings gives the first term at one setting and leaves out the second.

The settings are drawn by a random-walk Metropolis in unbounded coordinates: the log of a positive setting, the logit of a setting on an interval. The chain starts at the settings’ posterior mode, found by L-BFGS-B from the priors’ medians. It proposes steps from Laplace’s approximation there (the inverse of a numerical Hessian of the log posterior), and during the burn-in scales them toward an acceptance of 0.3 (Gelman, Roberts and Gilks, 1996); the scale is fixed after, so the kept steps are a Markov chain of the exact posterior. At each kept step it draws the state once, at that step’s settings. The effective sample size is Geyer’s (1992) initial monotone sequence.

On the fast path, with \(s\) the prior scale, \(o\) the observation scale and \(c = o^2 / \gamma\), the innovation covariance is \(s^2 H S_0 H^T + c S_z = s^2 (H S_0 H^T + S_z / \gamma')\) with \(\gamma' = s^2 / c\). The evidence at every \((s, c)\) is the problem’s at gamma \(\gamma'\), rescaled, and one eigendecomposition gives it at every gamma.

References#

Gelman, A., Roberts, G. O., and Gilks, W. R. (1996). Efficient Metropolis jumping rules. Bayesian Statistics 5, 599-607.

Geyer, C. J. (1992). Practical Markov chain Monte Carlo. Statistical Science, 7(4), 473-483.
