The uncertainty of the settings#

Fitting the covariance settings fits the covariance settings and solves at the fit, which treats them as known. The rule: give each setting a prior, and fips.hierarchical() draws the settings from their posterior and the state at each of those draws, so intervals read from its draws hold the uncertainty of the settings. The answer is not one Gaussian posterior but a mixture of them, one for each draw of the settings, held by a Hierarchical.

The settings’ posterior#

With a prior \(p(s)\) on the settings, their posterior is the prior times the evidence of the problem built at them (log_evidence, exact for a linear problem):

\[p(s \mid z) \propto p(s) \, p(z \mid s).\]

The posterior of the state is the posterior at each setting, averaged over the settings’ posterior,

\[p(x \mid z) = \int p(x \mid s, z) \, p(s \mid z) \, ds,\]

so a draw of the settings from \(p(s \mid z)\), then of the state from \(p(x \mid s, z)\) (exactly, sample()), is a draw of \(p(x \mid z)\). Its variance is

\[\operatorname{var}(x \mid z) = E\big[\hat{S}(s)\big] + \operatorname{var}\big[\hat{x}(s)\big],\]

over the settings’ posterior: the average of each setting’s posterior variance, and the spread of the estimates between settings. A solve at fitted settings gives the first term at one setting and leaves out the second.

fips.hierarchical() draws the settings by a random-walk Metropolis, in the log of each positive setting. It starts at the settings’ posterior mode, proposes steps from Laplace’s approximation there (the inverse of a numerical Hessian of the log posterior), and during the burn-in scales the steps until about 0.3 of them are accepted. Each step solves one problem; at each kept step it draws the state once, at that step’s settings.

A world at known settings#

The world of Fitting the covariance settings: a hundred and fifty cells along a line, 1 km apart, two hundred observations of one to four neighboring cells, and a truth drawn at a prior std of 1, a correlation length of 5 km and a mismatch std of 0.5:

In [1]: import numpy as np

In [2]: import pandas as pd

In [3]: from scipy.special import ndtri

In [4]: import fips

In [5]: rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

In [6]: n_cells, n_obs = 150, 200

In [7]: cells = fips.Space({"flux": pd.Index(np.arange(n_cells) + 0.5, name="x")})  # km

In [8]: observed = fips.Space({"ch4": pd.RangeIndex(n_obs, name="obs_id")})

In [9]: footprint = np.zeros((n_obs, n_cells))

In [10]: for i, start in enumerate(rng.integers(0, n_cells - 4, n_obs)):
   ....:     footprint[i, start : start + rng.integers(1, 5)] = rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5)
   ....: 

In [11]: H = fips.Operator(footprint, observed, cells)

In [12]: prior = fips.Vector(np.full(n_cells, 2.0), cells, name="prior")

The function from the settings to the problem takes them as a dict, by name:

In [13]: def build(settings, obs, H=H):
   ....:     """The problem at the settings: the prior std, the correlation length (km), the mismatch std."""
   ....:     return fips.Problem(
   ....:         prior=prior,
   ....:         prior_cov=fips.Exponential("x", settings["length"]).with_std(settings["std"]),
   ....:         obs=obs,
   ....:         obs_cov=fips.Independent().with_std(settings["noise"]),
   ....:         forward=H,
   ....:     )
   ....: 

In [14]: true = pd.Series({"std": 1.0, "length": 5.0, "noise": 0.5})

In [15]: world = build(true, fips.Vector(np.zeros(n_obs), observed))

In [16]: truth, obs = world.simulate(rng)

Each setting gets a prior that knows it to a factor of \(e\) (a LogNormal with a standard deviation of 1 in the log), about a guess that is off by a factor of two in the two standard deviations and 2.5 in the length. HalfNormal and Uniform are the other two priors fips has.

In [17]: priors = {
   ....:     "std": fips.LogNormal(np.log(0.5), 1.0),
   ....:     "length": fips.LogNormal(np.log(2.0), 1.0),
   ....:     "noise": fips.LogNormal(np.log(1.0), 1.0),
   ....: }
   ....: 

In [18]: result = fips.hierarchical(lambda s: build(s, obs), priors, draws=2000, burn_in=500, seed=0)

In [19]: table = result.settings.quantile([0.025, 0.5, 0.975]).T.set_axis(["2.5%", "median", "97.5%"], axis=1)

In [20]: table.insert(0, "true", true)

In [21]: table.assign(mode=result.mode).round(3)
Out[21]: 
         true   2.5%  median  97.5%   mode
setting                                   
std       1.0  0.789   0.983  1.275  0.965
length    5.0  1.761   2.811  5.818  2.751
noise     0.5  0.419   0.483  0.561  0.477

The two standard deviations are known to about 25% and 15% (their 95% intervals), each holding the truth. The length is known to a factor of three: from 1.8 to 5.8 km, about its median of 2.8 km, the fit Fitting the covariance settings found. mode is the settings’ posterior mode, where the chain started, close to that fit. The chain’s diagnostics are its acceptance and the effective sample size of each setting, about as many independent draws as the 2000 correlated ones are worth:

In [22]: round(result.acceptance, 2), result.ess.round().to_dict()
Out[22]: (0.26, {'std': 111.0, 'length': 122.0, 'noise': 199.0})

A standard error of a setting’s mean is its standard deviation over the root of its effective sample size: here about a tenth of its spread. Ask for more draws to make it smaller.

Totals with and without the settings’ uncertainty#

The flux of three regions of 50 km each: uncertainty() reads each total’s mean, standard deviation and interval from the draws (here 90%), and at() gives the ordinary posterior at one setting, the mode, whose interval leaves the settings’ uncertainty out:

In [23]: x = cells.select("flux")

In [24]: regions = pd.Series(np.select([x < 50, x < 100], ["west", "middle"], "east"), index=x, name="region")

In [25]: W = fips.aggregate.sum(cells, by=regions)

In [26]: def intervals(result, truth, level=0.9):
   ....:     """Each region's truth, and its interval with the settings' uncertainty and at their mode."""
   ....:     mixed = result.uncertainty(W, level=level)
   ....:     at_mode = result.at(result.mode)
   ....:     estimate = (W @ at_mode.estimate).values
   ....:     std = at_mode.uncertainty(W)["posterior_std"].values
   ....:     half = ndtri(0.5 + level / 2) * std
   ....:     return pd.DataFrame(
   ....:         {
   ....:             "truth": (W @ truth).values,
   ....:             "std": mixed["posterior_std"].values,
   ....:             "lower": mixed["lower"].values,
   ....:             "upper": mixed["upper"].values,
   ....:             "std_at_mode": std,
   ....:             "lower_at_mode": estimate - half,
   ....:             "upper_at_mode": estimate + half,
   ....:         },
   ....:         index=W.rows.index.get_level_values("region"),
   ....:     )
   ....: 

In [27]: intervals(result, truth).round(2)
Out[27]: 
         truth   std   lower   upper  std_at_mode  lower_at_mode  upper_at_mode
region                                                                         
east    109.10  1.98  106.68  113.30         1.95         106.80         113.20
middle  101.74  1.97   98.07  104.42         1.95          98.15         104.55
west    112.52  1.57  110.62  115.69         1.58         110.66         115.87

The two agree to about 2%: two hundred observations pin these totals, and the length, the setting known least, moves how a total splits between its cells more than the total itself (the prior std of each region’s total is 15.7; the posterior’s is under 2).

Few observations, and the fast path#

The settings’ uncertainty matters when few observations decide them. Fifteen observations, all in the western two regions:

In [28]: few = fips.Space({"ch4": pd.RangeIndex(15, name="obs_id")})

In [29]: sparse = np.zeros((15, n_cells))

In [30]: for i, start in enumerate(rng.integers(0, 96, 15)):
   ....:     sparse[i, start : start + rng.integers(1, 5)] = rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5)
   ....: 

In [31]: H_few = fips.Operator(sparse, few, cells)

In [32]: few_world = build(true, fips.Vector(np.zeros(15), few), H=H_few)

In [33]: truth_few, obs_few = few_world.simulate(rng)

Here the length is held at its true 5 km and the two standard deviations are the settings. When the only settings are a factor on the prior std and one on the mismatch’s, pass the problem at factors of 1 instead of a function, and name them "prior_scale" and "obs_scale" (or "gamma"): one eigendecomposition then gives the evidence at every step without a solve, and the state’s draws at every kept step in one product. This run of 2500 steps takes about a tenth of a second, where the function would take a solve each:

In [34]: unit = {"std": 1.0, "length": 5.0, "noise": 1.0}

In [35]: scales = {"prior_scale": fips.LogNormal(np.log(0.5), 1.0), "obs_scale": fips.LogNormal(0.0, 1.0)}

In [36]: fast = fips.hierarchical(build(unit, obs_few, H=H_few), scales, seed=0)

In [37]: fast.settings.quantile([0.025, 0.5, 0.975]).round(3)
Out[37]: 
setting  prior_scale  obs_scale
0.025          0.556      0.239
0.500          0.897      0.563
0.975          1.575      1.468

In [38]: intervals(fast, truth_few).round(2)
Out[38]: 
         truth    std  lower   upper  std_at_mode  lower_at_mode  upper_at_mode
region                                                                         
east     81.72  20.48  68.73  135.50        19.68          69.35         134.08
middle  108.50  10.85  97.83  132.99        10.36          98.15         132.23
west     74.70  13.27  63.47  106.72        12.16          63.54         103.54

Each standard deviation is now known to a factor of three to six, and the totals’ standard deviations with their uncertainty are 4% to 9% larger. Whether that is enough is a question of coverage: over worlds, how often each interval holds the true total. First in worlds at the true settings, then in worlds whose settings are drawn from their priors, 200 of each:

In [39]: def coverage(make_world, trials=200):
   ....:     """The fraction of worlds whose 90% interval holds each region's true total."""
   ....:     rows = []
   ....:     for k in range(trials):
   ....:         truth_k, obs_k = make_world()
   ....:         table = intervals(fips.hierarchical(build(unit, obs_k, H=H_few), scales, seed=k), truth_k)
   ....:         rows.append(pd.DataFrame({
   ....:             "with": table["lower"].le(table["truth"]) & table["truth"].le(table["upper"]),
   ....:             "without": table["lower_at_mode"].le(table["truth"]) & table["truth"].le(table["upper_at_mode"]),
   ....:         }))
   ....:     return pd.concat(rows).groupby(level="region").mean()
   ....: 

In [40]: coverage(lambda: few_world.simulate(rng))
Out[40]: 
         with  without
region                
east    0.845    0.825
middle  0.860    0.835
west    0.885    0.845

In [41]: def from_priors():
   ....:     """A world whose two standard deviations are drawn from their priors."""
   ....:     std, noise = (float(scales[name].sample(1, rng)[0]) for name in ("prior_scale", "obs_scale"))
   ....:     world_k = build({"std": std, "length": 5.0, "noise": noise}, fips.Vector(np.zeros(15), few), H=H_few)
   ....:     return world_k.simulate(rng)
   ....: 

In [42]: coverage(from_priors)
Out[42]: 
         with  without
region                
east    0.935    0.885
middle  0.895    0.875
west    0.890    0.850

With 200 worlds a coverage is known to about 0.02. In the worlds drawn from the priors, the intervals with the settings’ uncertainty cover at their level, 89% to 93.5%, as a posterior must on average over its own prior; those at the mode cover 85% to 89%. At the true settings both cover less, 84.5% to 88.5% with the uncertainty and 82.5% to 84.5% without: there the priors pull each standard deviation towards their median, the prior std’s (0.5) below the truth and the mismatch std’s (1) above it, and fifteen observations do not undo all of the pull. With the prior std’s prior median at the truth instead, the same worlds cover 87% to 91.5% with the uncertainty (a run not on this page). A prior on the settings is a claim like any other, and the intervals are only as good as it.

What it costs, and what it does not do#

A step of the general path is a solve of the problem the function builds (here 4 ms for 150 cells and 200 observations, so the 2500 steps above took about 12 s), and the search for the mode and its Hessian about sixty more. A step of the fast path is \(O(m)\) (or \(O(n)\), whichever is smaller) after one eigendecomposition, whatever the size of the problem. The draws of the state are kept, a state x draws array: 2000 draws of 30,000 cells are 480 MB. The settings’ estimates have Monte Carlo errors that ess measures; four hundred effective draws give a setting’s mean to a twentieth of its spread.

The posterior of the state is a mixture, so what is defined for one Gaussian (the gain, DOFS, the chi-squared tests, the evidence) is read from the posterior at one setting, result.at(settings). The method is the analytic one: a function that returns a "bounded" or "gauss_newton" posterior is refused, since neither gives the exact evidence and exact draws each step needs.
