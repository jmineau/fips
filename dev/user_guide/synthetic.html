Synthetic experiments#

An inversion returns an estimate and a claim about how uncertain it is. The one way to check both is to give it data whose answer you know: make a truth, make the observations it would give, solve them, and compare. problem.simulate(rng) makes a truth and its observations, and post.with_data(obs) solves them for the cost of a product. post.recovery(world, W=W) gives the answer of every such trial at once, exactly: the bias, the spread and the coverage of each aggregate, one row each. post.trials(world, n, rng, W=W) gives the same by sampling, trial by trial, for what has no exact answer.

Atmospheric science calls this an observing system simulation experiment (OSSE), oceanography a twin experiment, and statistics a simulation study. fips uses the statistician’s parts, because every such experiment has the same four:

Part

What it is

In fips

world

What makes the data: a truth and the observations it gives. Also called the data-generating mechanism or the nature run.

A Problem, through simulate(); or any function of a generator that returns a truth and observations.

aggregates

What you compare on: a monthly total, a regional mean, a difference between seasons, or each state element. Also called the estimands.

An aggregation W (fips.aggregate.sum(), mean()), one row per aggregate.

inversion

The method under test, with its own assumptions. Also called the estimator.

A Posterior; post.with_data(obs) runs it on other data.

performance

How the estimate’s error behaves over worlds: its bias, its spread, the spread the inversion claims, and how often the claimed interval holds the truth.

The columns of recovery(), exactly; or the same columns summarized from the table of trials().

The design is whether the world’s assumptions and the inversion’s agree. When the world is the problem itself, the experiment asks whether the inversion is honest. When one input of the world differs from what the inversion assumes, it asks what that input costs.

An example problem#

Three months of a quantity at four positions along a line, observed 24 times, each observation a weighted sum of the positions in its own month. The state has a month level and an x level, so a monthly total is a sum over x:

In [1]: import numpy as np

In [2]: import pandas as pd

In [3]: import fips

In [4]: rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

In [5]: months = pd.date_range("2024-01-01", periods=3, freq="MS", name="month")

In [6]: positions = pd.Index([0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5], name="x")

In [7]: state = pd.MultiIndex.from_product([months, positions])

In [8]: prior = fips.Vector.from_series(pd.Series(1.0, index=state, name="prior"), block="field")

In [9]: obs_space = fips.Space({"obs": pd.RangeIndex(24, name="obs_id")})

In [10]: weights = rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0, (24, 12))

In [11]: own_month = np.repeat(np.arange(3), 8)[:, None] == np.repeat(np.arange(3), 4)[None, :]

In [12]: forward = fips.Operator(weights * own_month, obs_space, prior.space)

A synthetic experiment leans on the function that builds the problem (every repeated inversion has one, Repeated inversions, logging and saving): a world with one input changed is the same call with one argument changed. The observations are zeros, because a world makes the real ones and the inversion needs only their labels:

In [13]: def build_problem(obs_std=0.3, offset=0.0, prior_std=0.5):
   ....:     """Return the problem, with the given observation error, offset and prior spread."""
   ....:     prior_cov = fips.Kronecker(
   ....:         fips.Exponential("month", "45D"), fips.Exponential("x", 1.0)
   ....:     ).with_std(prior_std)
   ....:     return fips.Problem(
   ....:         prior=prior,
   ....:         prior_cov=prior_cov,
   ....:         obs=fips.Vector(np.zeros(24), obs_space, name="obs"),
   ....:         obs_cov=fips.Independent().with_std(obs_std),
   ....:         forward=forward,
   ....:         offset=offset,
   ....:     )
   ....: 

In [14]: problem = build_problem()

In [15]: post = fips.solve(problem)

In [16]: W = fips.aggregate.sum(prior.space, by="month")  # one row per monthly total

A world makes the data#

A Problem is a world. simulate() draws a truth from its prior, \(x \sim N(x_0, S_0)\), and the observations that truth gives, \(z = H x + c + e\) with \(e \sim N(0, S_z)\), and returns the two as vectors (predict() is the same without the error). The inversion then solves the world’s data with post.with_data(obs), which costs one product, not a new solve:

In [17]: truth, obs = problem.simulate(rng)

In [18]: trial = post.with_data(obs)

In [19]: miss = (W @ trial.estimate - W @ truth).values

In [20]: claimed = np.sqrt(post.cov(W).variances.values)

In [21]: pd.DataFrame({"miss": miss, "claimed_std": claimed}, index=months).round(3)
Out[21]: 
             miss  claimed_std
month                         
2024-01-01  0.183        0.203
2024-02-01 -0.151        0.225
2024-03-01 -0.128        0.205

One trial says little. Its point is the shape of every experiment: the world makes the data, the inversion solves them with its own assumptions, and the aggregates of the two are compared. There are four kinds of world:

  • The problem itself, problem.simulate(rng): the inversion’s assumptions are true. The test is whether it is honest.

  • A chosen truth, problem.simulate(rng, truth=x): another prior estimate, or 1.5 times the prior, on the prior’s labels in any order. Only the observation error is drawn. The test is how much of that truth the inversion recovers.

  • One input wrong, build_problem(obs_std=1.2).simulate(rng): the builder with one argument changed (the observation error, the offset, the prior spread, a forward operator scaled by a model error). The inversion keeps its own assumptions; the test is what the wrong input costs.

  • Another observing system: a separate problem with other observations and its own solve, compared on the uncertainty of the aggregates (uncertainty(), data_worth()). That comparison needs no synthetic data.

A world whose truth is not a Gaussian draw, or whose error changes from trial to trial in a way no covariance describes, is a function: Worlds no Problem can describe.

The exact answer#

For the analytic method, recovery() gives the answer of every trial at once. The estimate is linear in the observations, so when a Gaussian world makes the data, the error of every aggregate is Gaussian too, with a mean and a spread in closed form: no trials, no sampling noise. One row per aggregate:

In [22]: honest = post.recovery(W=W).droplevel("block")

In [23]: honest.round(3)
Out[23]: 
            prior  truth  estimate  bias    std  posterior_std  coverage
month                                                                   
2024-01-01    4.0    4.0       4.0   0.0  0.203          0.203      0.95
2024-02-01    4.0    4.0       4.0   0.0  0.225          0.225      0.95
2024-03-01    4.0    4.0       4.0   0.0  0.205          0.205      0.95

The columns are the performance measures of a simulation study:

  • bias: the mean of estimate - truth, zero when the inversion’s assumptions hold at a gamma of 1;

  • std: the real spread of the error over worlds (the empirical standard error);

  • posterior_std: the spread the inversion claims (the model standard error, \(\sqrt{\operatorname{diag} W \hat{S} W^T}\));

  • coverage: the probability that the posterior’s interval at level (0.95 by default) holds the truth.

In its own world, as here, the bias is zero, std equals posterior_std, and the coverage is the level. Any other world is the same call with the world as the first argument. A world whose observation error is four times what the inversion assumes makes the inversion overconfident: the real spread is larger than the claimed one, and the interval holds the truth far less often than it claims:

In [24]: noisy = build_problem(obs_std=1.2)

In [25]: post.recovery(noisy, W=W).droplevel("block")[["bias", "std", "posterior_std", "coverage"]].round(3)
Out[25]: 
            bias    std  posterior_std  coverage
month                                           
2024-01-01   0.0  0.772          0.203     0.393
2024-02-01   0.0  0.837          0.225     0.401
2024-03-01   0.0  0.794          0.205     0.388

A chosen truth fixes the state and leaves only the observation error random. With 1.5 times the prior, the bias is the part of the change the inversion does not recover, and (estimate - prior) / (truth - prior) is the fraction it does. A second inversion, whose prior spread is five times smaller, shows what a tight prior costs: it pulls the estimate back toward a prior it trusts, and recovers less of a change it did not expect:

In [26]: bigger = 1.5 * prior

In [27]: post_tight = fips.solve(build_problem(prior_std=0.1))

In [28]: def recovered(post):
   ....:     """Return the fraction of the change from the prior to `bigger` that `post` recovers."""
   ....:     table = post.recovery(truth=bigger, W=W).droplevel("block")
   ....:     return (table["estimate"] - table["prior"]) / (table["truth"] - table["prior"])
   ....: 

In [29]: pd.DataFrame({"loose prior": recovered(post), "tight prior": recovered(post_tight)}).round(3)
Out[29]: 
            loose prior  tight prior
month                               
2024-01-01        0.980        0.743
2024-02-01        0.991        0.802
2024-03-01        0.981        0.737

recovery needs a world whose forward map is an operator, with the posterior’s observation labels, and a Gaussian or fixed truth. It needs an estimate linear in the observations, which only the analytic method gives (the members table); any other method raises UnavailableError. A world whose state has other labels is compared on aggregates both states add up to (A world on other state labels). Everything else is a loop of trials.

When you need trials#

Three things have no exact answer: a statistic recovery does not give (the reduced chi-squared of each trial), a world whose truth is not Gaussian or whose error is not additive, and a method whose estimate is not linear in the observations. For those, sample. post.trials is the loop recovery answers exactly, kept trial by trial. It takes the same world, W and W_world, plus the number of trials and a seed. In each trial the world makes the data, the inversion solves them with with_data, and the aggregates of the two go into one row per aggregate:

In [30]: table = post.trials(problem, 400, rng=0, W=W)

In [31]: table.head(6)
Out[31]:

trial block month                                                                          
0     field 2024-01-01  4.461368  4.261741       0.202810  3.864241  4.659241      0.671470
            2024-02-01  5.188488  5.270503       0.224721  4.830058  5.710948      0.671470
            2024-03-01  3.085033  3.099524       0.205108  2.697520  3.501527      0.671470
1     field 2024-01-01  4.594656  4.688300       0.202810  4.290800  5.085800      1.303863
            2024-02-01  5.422497  5.264394       0.224721  4.823949  5.704839      1.303863
            2024-03-01  6.299730  6.373827       0.205108  5.971824  6.775830      1.303863

The table is long: one row per trial and aggregate, with the spread the posterior claims, its interval (lower and upper, as uncertainty() gives them: the kept draws’ quantiles for a sampled posterior) and the trial’s reduced chi-squared. pandas does the rest. The performance measures are a groupby over the aggregates, each with its Monte Carlo standard error (Morris, White and Crowther, 2019): with n trials a bias is known to std / sqrt(n), and a coverage near 0.95 to about 0.22 / sqrt(n).

In [32]: def performance(table):
   ....:     """Return recovery's columns from a table of trials, with their Monte Carlo standard errors."""
   ....:     by = [name for name in table.index.names if name != "trial"]  # the aggregates
   ....:     def per_row(values):  # dropna=False: a level a block lacks is NaN
   ....:         return values.groupby(level=by, dropna=False)
   ....:     error = table["estimate"] - table["truth"]
   ....:     inside = (table["lower"] <= table["truth"]) & (table["truth"] <= table["upper"])
   ....:     out = pd.DataFrame({
   ....:         "bias": per_row(error).mean(),
   ....:         "std": per_row(error).std(),
   ....:         "posterior_std": per_row(table["posterior_std"]).mean(),
   ....:         "coverage": per_row(inside).mean(),
   ....:         "reduced_chi2": per_row(table["reduced_chi2"]).mean(),
   ....:     })
   ....:     n = per_row(error).size()
   ....:     return out.assign(bias_se=out["std"] / np.sqrt(n), coverage_se=np.sqrt(out["coverage"] * (1 - out["coverage"]) / n))
   ....:

The honest world, sampled and exact, side by side. The sampled coverage is within its standard error of the exact one, and the reduced chi-squared averages 1, the one number the exact table does not have:

In [33]: sampled = performance(table).droplevel("block")

Worlds no Problem can describe#

A world is anything that takes a generator and returns a truth and observations. Problem.simulate is one; a function of your own is another, and trials takes either. Two worlds an experiment often needs and a problem cannot describe: a truth that is lognormal about the prior (so it stays positive and its spread scales with the prior), and an error the inversion cannot see, such as a model error that scales every observation of a trial by one factor. Each is a few lines, because vectors do arithmetic by label:

In [37]: field = fips.Kronecker(fips.Exponential("month", "45D"), fips.Exponential("x", 1.0)).with_std(0.4)

In [38]: field_cov = field.build(prior.space)

In [39]: half_var = 0.5 * field_cov.variances  # so the truth's mean is the prior

In [40]: def lognormal_world(rng):
   ....:     """Return a lognormal truth about the prior, and its observations with a 10% model error the inversion cannot see."""
   ....:     truth = prior * np.exp(field_cov.sample(rng) - half_var)
   ....:     scale = np.exp(rng.normal(0.0, 0.10))
   ....:     obs = problem.predict(truth) * scale + problem.obs_cov.sample(rng)
   ....:     return truth, obs
   ....: 

In [41]: performance(post.trials(lognormal_world, 400, rng=0, W=W)).droplevel("block")[["bias", "std", "posterior_std", "coverage"]].round(3)
Out[41]: 
             bias    std  posterior_std  coverage
month                                            
2024-01-01  0.032  0.436          0.203     0.662
2024-02-01  0.059  0.439          0.225     0.698
2024-03-01  0.040  0.442          0.205     0.665

The error the inversion cannot see lands in the aggregates: the real spread is well above the claimed one, and the interval holds the truth much less often than it claims.

A truth with a chosen amplitude, a seasonal cycle or a factor per part of the state is the same kind of function. What the world does is yours to write. What fips gives is a draw from any of its covariance models (Covariance.sample), the noise-free observations of any truth (predict()), and the solve of any data for the cost of a product (with_data()).

Two inversions on the same data#

To compare two inversions, give them the same data. The world does not depend on the inversion, so the same seed gives both the same truths and the same observations, trial for trial, and pd.concat with a key pairs them. Here the loose prior and the tight one, in the honest world of the loose one:

In [42]: both = pd.concat({"loose": post.trials(problem, 400, rng=0, W=W), "tight": post_tight.trials(problem, 400, rng=0, W=W)}, names=["inversion"])

In [43]: performance(both).droplevel("block")[["bias", "std", "posterior_std", "coverage"]].round(3)
Out[43]: 
                       bias    std  posterior_std  coverage
inversion month                                            
loose     2024-01-01  0.007  0.202          0.203     0.960
          2024-02-01  0.002  0.220          0.225     0.952
          2024-03-01 -0.007  0.201          0.205     0.948
tight     2024-01-01 -0.020  0.473          0.150     0.462
          2024-02-01  0.028  0.509          0.149     0.420
          2024-03-01 -0.022  0.493          0.153     0.425

The tight inversion has no bias on average, but it claims much less spread than it has, so its interval holds the truth far less often than it claims: the prior it trusts is not the one the world drew from. Comparing ways to keep a quantity positive pairs four methods this way.

A world on other state labels#

Two inversions whose states differ are compared on aggregates both add up to. A fine and a coarse grid, a monthly and an annual state, the whole state and the part a solve was restricted to: each state has its own aggregation to the same rows. recovery and trials take the world’s as W_world, and the error of each aggregate, \(W \hat{x} - W_w x\), keeps its closed form.

For example: does an annual inversion, fed the observations of a truth with a season, recover the mean, or does it alias the season through weights that are larger in some months? The annual inversion has one value per position, and its forward operator is the monthly one with each position’s three columns added. The aggregates are each position’s value: the identity on the annual state, the mean over the months on the monthly one.

In [44]: annual_space = fips.Space({"field": positions})

In [45]: per_position = np.kron(np.ones((3, 1)), np.eye(4))  # a monthly element -> its position

In [46]: annual = fips.Problem(
   ....:     prior=fips.Vector(np.ones(4), annual_space, name="prior"),
   ....:     prior_cov=fips.Exponential("x", 1.0).with_std(0.5),
   ....:     obs=fips.Vector(np.zeros(24), obs_space, name="obs"),
   ....:     obs_cov=fips.Independent().with_std(0.3),
   ....:     forward=fips.Operator((weights * own_month) @ per_position, obs_space, annual_space),
   ....: )
   ....: 

In [47]: post_annual = fips.solve(annual)

In [48]: W_annual = fips.aggregate.sum(annual_space, by="x")  # each position's value

In [49]: W_monthly = fips.aggregate.mean(prior.space, by="x")  # the mean over the months

A truth of 1.5 times the prior, flat or with a season (one month at 1.3 times its mean, the other two at 0.85), with no observation error, against the same truth recovered by the monthly inversion and averaged over the months:

In [50]: def aliasing(season):
   ....:     """Return the bias of each position's value from the annual and the monthly inversion."""
   ....:     truth = fips.Vector(1.5 * prior.values * np.repeat(season, 4), prior.space)
   ....:     annual_bias = post_annual.recovery(problem, truth=truth, W=W_annual, W_world=W_monthly)["bias"]
   ....:     monthly_bias = post.recovery(truth=truth, W=W_monthly)["bias"]
   ....:     return pd.DataFrame({"annual": annual_bias, "monthly, averaged": monthly_bias}).droplevel("block")
   ....: 

In [51]: pd.concat({"flat": aliasing([1.0, 1.0, 1.0]), "first month high": aliasing([1.3, 0.85, 0.85])}, axis=1).round(3)

Reading the table#

Two more questions an experiment asks are read off the table of trials with pandas.

  • Where does the real estimate fall among worlds consistent with the prior? Run the honest world and find the quantile of the real estimate in each aggregate’s estimate column. A real value that almost no honest world reaches says the truth was not within the prior’s spread.

  • What truth is consistent with the estimate? Regress the truth on the estimate over the trials and read the fit at the real value; the quantiles of the residuals are its interval. For the tight inversion the slope is above 1, because its estimates move less than the truth does, and the fit undoes that shrinkage:

In [54]: tight = both.xs("tight", level="inversion")

In [55]: X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(tight)), tight["estimate"]])

In [56]: beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, tight["truth"], rcond=None)

In [57]: residual = tight["truth"] - X @ beta

In [58]: np.round(beta, 3), np.round(np.percentile(residual, [16, 84]), 3)
Out[58]: (array([-1.556,  1.389]), array([-0.327,  0.323]))

With two inversions paired on the same worlds, the same regression on both estimates reads the truth off the pair.

Randomness#

Every draw takes rng: a seed, a numpy.random.Generator, or None for fresh entropy, through numpy.random.default_rng(), as SciPy’s rng argument does. One generator passed to every call of a loop gives independent trials. A seed gives the same draws again for the same covariance forms; a Kronecker covariance on part of its grid draws on the whole grid, so changing a model’s form changes the draws. For trials in parallel, spawn one generator per worker, np.random.SeedSequence(seed).spawn(k), and keep each worker’s trials in the table with a worker key, so the run is reproducible whatever the scheduling.

How it works#

The analytic estimate is linear in the observations, \(\hat{x} = x_0 + K (z - H x_0 - c)\), with the gain \(K\) of the posterior (its problem’s \(H\), \(c\), \(S_0\) and \(S_z / \gamma\)). When a world makes the data, with a truth \(x \sim N(\mu, P)\) (its prior, or a given truth with \(P = 0\)) and observations \(z = H_w x + c_w + e\), \(e \sim N(0, R_w)\) (its forward operator, offset and observation covariance), the error \(\hat{x} - x\) is Gaussian, with mean and covariance

\[b = (x_0 - \mu) + K (H_w \mu + c_w - H x_0 - c), \qquad V = (K H_w - I) P (K H_w - I)^T + K R_w K^T.\]

recovery reports \(W b\) and the root of the diagonal of \(W V W^T\), and the coverage of an error \(N(\text{bias}, \text{std}^2)\) by the interval \(\pm z_{level}\) posterior_std. It computes them from \(W K\) and \(W K H_w - W\), with \(P\) and \(R_w\) applied in their own forms, so it forms nothing \(n \times n\) or \(m \times m\) beyond what the posterior holds. When the world is the problem itself and gamma is 1, \(b = 0\) and \(V = \hat{S}\). With W_world, the error of an aggregate is \(W \hat{x} - W_w x\), and the same formula holds with each side’s own \(W\).

trials makes the data with the world, solves them with with_data() (one product with the factors, because they depend on where the observations are and how uncertain they are, not on their values) and keeps each trial’s aggregates. For a method whose estimate is not linear in the observations, each trial’s claimed spread and interval are its own posterior’s.
