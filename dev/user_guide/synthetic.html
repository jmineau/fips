Synthetic experiments#

An inversion returns an estimate and a claim about how uncertain it is. The one way to check both is to give it data whose answer you know: make a truth, make the observations it would produce, solve them, and compare. Atmospheric science calls this an OSSE (an observing system simulation experiment), oceanography a twin experiment, and statistics a simulation study. fips uses the statistician’s parts, because every such experiment has the same four:

Part

What it is

In fips

world

What makes the data: a truth and the observations it gives. Also called the data-generating mechanism or the nature run.

A Problem, through simulate(); or any function of a generator that returns a truth and observations.

totals

What you compare on: monthly totals, a regional total, a seasonal difference, or each state element. Also called the estimands.

An aggregation W (fips.aggregate.sum()), one row per total.

inversion

The method under test, with its own assumptions. Also called the estimator.

A Posterior; post.with_data(obs) runs it on other data.

performance

How the estimate’s error behaves over worlds: its bias, its spread, the spread the inversion claims, and how often the claimed interval holds the truth.

The columns of recovery(), exactly; or the same columns summarized from a table of trials.

The inversion’s assumptions and the world’s may agree or differ, and that is the whole design space. When the world is the problem itself, the experiment asks whether the inversion is honest. When one input of the world differs from what the inversion assumes, it asks what that input costs. This page builds a small problem, makes worlds for it, reads the exact answer where there is one, and writes the loop where there is not.

A problem, and the function that builds it#

Three months of fluxes in four cells along a line, observed by 24 measurements, each seeing the cells of its own month with random weights. The state has a month level and an x level, so monthly totals are a sum over x:

In [1]: import numpy as np

In [2]: import pandas as pd

In [3]: from scipy.stats import norm

In [4]: import fips

In [5]: rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

In [6]: months = pd.date_range("2024-01-01", periods=3, freq="MS", name="month")

In [7]: cells = pd.Index([0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5], name="x")  # km

In [8]: state = pd.MultiIndex.from_product([months, cells])

In [9]: prior = fips.Vector.from_series(pd.Series(1.0, index=state, name="prior"), block="flux")

In [10]: obs_labels = pd.Index(range(24), name="obs_id")

In [11]: obs_space = fips.Space({"conc": obs_labels})

In [12]: weights = rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0, (24, 12))

In [13]: sees_month = np.repeat(np.arange(3), 8)[:, None] == np.repeat(np.arange(3), 4)[None, :]

In [14]: forward = fips.Operator(weights * sees_month, obs_space, prior.space)

The builder is the function that makes a Problem from its inputs. Every repeated inversion has one (Repeated inversions, logging and saving), and a synthetic experiment leans on it: a world with one input changed is the same call with one argument changed. The observations are zeros, because a world makes the real ones and the inversion only needs their labels:

In [15]: def build_problem(obs_std=0.3, offset=0.0, prior_std=0.5):
   ....:     """Return the problem, with the given mismatch, background and prior spread."""
   ....:     prior_cov = fips.Kronecker(
   ....:         fips.Exponential("month", "45D"), fips.Exponential("x", 1.0)
   ....:     ).with_std(prior_std)
   ....:     return fips.Problem(
   ....:         prior=prior,
   ....:         prior_cov=prior_cov,
   ....:         obs=fips.Vector(np.zeros(24), obs_space, name="conc"),
   ....:         obs_cov=fips.Independent().with_std(obs_std),
   ....:         forward=forward,
   ....:         offset=offset,
   ....:     )
   ....: 

In [16]: problem = build_problem()

In [17]: post = fips.solve(problem)

In [18]: W = fips.aggregate.sum(prior.space, by="month")  # the totals: one row per month

A world makes the data#

A Problem is a world through simulate(): it draws a truth from its prior, \(x \sim N(x_0, S_0)\), and the observations that truth gives, \(z = H x + c + e\) with \(e \sim N(0, S_z)\), and returns the two as vectors. predict() is the same without the error. The draws are Covariance.sample, which draws from \(N(0, S)\) without forming \(S\) where its form has a cheaper root (a Kronecker covariance draws through its factors). The inversion then runs on the world’s data with post.with_data(obs), which costs one product, not a new solve, because the factorization depends on where the observations are and how uncertain they are, not on their values:

In [19]: truth, obs = problem.simulate(rng)

In [20]: trial = post.with_data(obs)

In [21]: miss = (W @ trial.estimate - W @ truth).values

In [22]: claimed = np.sqrt(post.cov(W).variances.values)

In [23]: pd.DataFrame({"miss": miss, "claimed_std": claimed}, index=months).round(3)
Out[23]: 
             miss  claimed_std
month                         
2024-01-01  0.183        0.203
2024-02-01 -0.151        0.225
2024-03-01 -0.128        0.205

One trial says little; its point is the shape of every experiment: the world makes the data, the inversion solves them with its own assumptions, and the totals of the two are compared. There are four kinds of world, and each is a Problem or a given truth:

  • The problem itself, problem.simulate(rng): the inversion’s assumptions are true. The test is whether it is honest.

  • A chosen truth, problem.simulate(rng, truth=inventory): another inventory, or 1.5 times the prior, on the prior’s labels in any order. Only the observation error is drawn. The test is how much of that truth is recovered.

  • One input wrong, build_problem(obs_std=1.2).simulate(rng): your builder with one argument changed (the mismatch, the background, the prior spread, a forward operator scaled by a transport error). The inversion keeps its assumptions; the test is what the wrong input costs.

  • Another observing system: a separate Problem with other observations and its own solve, compared on the uncertainty of the totals (uncertainty(), data_worth()). No synthetic data is needed for that comparison.

A world whose truth is not a Gaussian draw, or whose error changes from trial to trial in a way no covariance describes, is a function: Worlds no Problem can describe.

The exact answer#

The analytic method’s estimate is linear in the observations, \(\hat{x} = x_0 + K (z - H x_0 - c)\), so when a Gaussian world makes the data, the error of every total is Gaussian too, and its mean and covariance have a closed form. recovery() returns that form, one row per total: no trials, no sampling noise.

In [24]: honest = post.recovery(W=W).droplevel("block")

In [25]: honest.round(3)
Out[25]: 
            prior  truth  estimate  bias    std  posterior_std  coverage
month                                                                   
2024-01-01    4.0    4.0       4.0   0.0  0.203          0.203      0.95
2024-02-01    4.0    4.0       4.0   0.0  0.225          0.225      0.95
2024-03-01    4.0    4.0       4.0   0.0  0.205          0.205      0.95

The columns are the performance measures of a simulation study:

  • bias, the mean of estimate - truth: zero when the inversion’s assumptions hold at gamma 1.

  • std, the real spread of the error over worlds (the empirical standard error).

  • posterior_std, the spread the inversion claims (the model standard error, \(\sqrt{\operatorname{diag} W \hat{S} W^T}\)).

  • coverage, the probability that the posterior’s interval at level holds the truth: 0.95 for an honest inversion at the default level.

In its own world the bias is zero, std equals posterior_std, and the coverage is the level. Each other world is the same call with the world as the first argument. The world’s mismatch four times what the inversion assumes makes the inversion overconfident: the real spread is larger than the claimed one, and the 95% interval holds the truth far less often than claimed:

In [26]: noisy = build_problem(obs_std=1.2)

In [27]: post.recovery(noisy, W=W).droplevel("block")[["bias", "std", "posterior_std", "coverage"]].round(3)
Out[27]: 
            bias    std  posterior_std  coverage
month                                           
2024-01-01   0.0  0.772          0.203     0.393
2024-02-01   0.0  0.837          0.225     0.401
2024-03-01   0.0  0.794          0.205     0.388

A chosen truth fixes the state and leaves only the observation error random. With 1.5 times the prior, the bias is the part of the change the inversion does not recover, and (estimate - prior) / (truth - prior) is the recovered fraction. A second inversion, whose prior is trusted five times more, shows what regularization costs: it pulls the estimate back toward the prior it believes, and recovers about three quarters of a change it did not expect where the first recovers nearly all of it:

In [28]: bigger = 1.5 * prior

In [29]: post_tight = fips.solve(build_problem(prior_std=0.1))

In [30]: def recovered(post):
   ....:     """Return the fraction of the change from the prior to `bigger` that `post` recovers."""
   ....:     table = post.recovery(truth=bigger, W=W).droplevel("block")
   ....:     return (table["estimate"] - table["prior"]) / (table["truth"] - table["prior"])
   ....: 

In [31]: pd.DataFrame({"loose prior": recovered(post), "tight prior": recovered(post_tight)}).round(3)
Out[31]: 
            loose prior  tight prior
month                               
2024-01-01        0.980        0.743
2024-02-01        0.991        0.802
2024-03-01        0.981        0.737

recovery takes a world with the posterior’s observation labels, a method whose estimate is linear in the observations, and a Gaussian or fixed truth. A world whose state has other labels is compared on totals that both add up to (A world on other state labels). Everything else is a loop.

When you need trials#

Three things have no closed form: a statistic recovery does not give (the reduced chi-squared of each trial), a world whose truth is not Gaussian or whose error is not additive, and a method whose estimate is not linear in the observations. For those, write the loop. It is short, and it is yours: the world makes the data, the inversion solves them, and each trial adds its rows to a table.

In [32]: def trials(world, post, n, rng, W):
   ....:     """Return one row per trial and total: the truth, the estimate, the claimed std, chi2."""
   ....:     rng = np.random.default_rng(rng)
   ....:     make = world.simulate if isinstance(world, fips.Problem) else world
   ....:     claimed = np.sqrt(post.cov(W).variances.values)
   ....:     rows = []
   ....:     for i in range(n):
   ....:         truth, obs = make(rng)
   ....:         trial = post.with_data(obs)
   ....:         rows.append(pd.DataFrame({"trial": i, "truth": (W @ truth).values,
   ....:                                   "estimate": (W @ trial.estimate).values,
   ....:                                   "posterior_std": claimed, "chi2": trial.reduced_chi2},
   ....:                                  index=W.rows.index))
   ....:     return pd.concat(rows).set_index("trial", append=True)
   ....:

The table is long: one row per trial and total, so pandas does the rest. The same performance measures come from a groupby over the totals, with the Monte Carlo standard error of each (Morris, White and Crowther, 2019): with n trials a bias is known to std / sqrt(n) and a coverage near 0.95 to about 0.22 / sqrt(n).

In [33]: def performance(table, level=0.95):
   ....:     """Return recovery's columns from a table of trials, with their Monte Carlo standard errors."""
   ....:     z = norm.ppf(1 - (1 - level) / 2)
   ....:     error = table["estimate"] - table["truth"]
   ....:     inside = error.abs() <= z * table["posterior_std"]
   ....:     by = table.index.names[:-1]
   ....:     n = error.groupby(level=by).size()
   ....:     out = pd.DataFrame({
   ....:         "bias": error.groupby(level=by).mean(),
   ....:         "std": error.groupby(level=by).std(),
   ....:         "posterior_std": table["posterior_std"].groupby(level=by).first(),
   ....:         "coverage": inside.groupby(level=by).mean(),
   ....:         "chi2": table["chi2"].groupby(level=by).mean(),
   ....:     })
   ....:     out["bias_se"] = out["std"] / np.sqrt(n)
   ....:     out["coverage_se"] = np.sqrt(out["coverage"] * (1 - out["coverage"]) / n)
   ....:     return out
   ....:

The honest world, sampled and exact, side by side. The sampled coverage is within its standard error of 0.95, and the reduced chi-squared averages 1, the one number the exact table does not have:

In [34]: sampled = performance(trials(problem, post, 400, 0, W)).droplevel("block")

In [35]: pd.DataFrame({"sampled": sampled["coverage"], "exact": honest["coverage"],
   ....:               "se": sampled["coverage_se"]}).round(3)
   ....:

Use the exact table where it applies and the loop for what it lacks; where both apply, the loop should agree with the table within its standard errors, which is also the check that your loop is right.

Worlds no Problem can describe#

A world is anything that takes a generator and returns a truth and observations. Problem.simulate is one; a function of your own is another, and the loop above takes either. Two worlds a real experiment needs and a Problem cannot describe: a truth that is lognormal about the prior (so it stays positive and its spread scales with the prior), and an error the inversion cannot see, such as a transport error that scales every observation of a trial by one factor. Each is a few lines, since vectors do arithmetic by label:

In [38]: field = fips.Kronecker(fips.Exponential("month", "45D"), fips.Exponential("x", 1.0)).with_std(0.4)

In [39]: field_cov = field.build(prior.space)

In [40]: half_var = 0.5 * field_cov.variances  # so the truth's mean is the prior

In [41]: def lognormal_world(rng):
   ....:     """A lognormal truth about the prior, and a transport error of 10% the inversion cannot see."""
   ....:     truth = prior * np.exp(field_cov.sample(rng) - half_var)
   ....:     scale = np.exp(rng.normal(0.0, 0.10))
   ....:     obs = problem.predict(truth) * scale + problem.obs_cov.sample(rng)
   ....:     return truth, obs
   ....: 

In [42]: performance(trials(lognormal_world, post, 400, 0, W)).droplevel("block")[["bias", "std", "posterior_std", "coverage"]].round(3)
Out[42]: 
             bias    std  posterior_std  coverage
month                                            
2024-01-01  0.032  0.436          0.203     0.662
2024-02-01  0.059  0.439          0.225     0.698
2024-03-01  0.040  0.442          0.205     0.665

The error the inversion cannot see lands in the totals: the real spread is twice the claimed one, and the 95% interval holds the truth two times in three. The bias is small and positive because the transport factor averages a little above one (it is the exponential of a zero-mean normal).

A truth with a chosen amplitude, a seasonal cycle or a sector-by-sector factor is the same kind of function. What the world does is yours to write; what fips gives is the draw from any of its covariance models (Covariance.sample), the noise-free observations of any truth (predict()), and the solve of any data at the cost of a product (with_data()).

Two inversions on the same data#

To compare two inversions, give them the same data. The world does not depend on the inversion, so the same seed gives both the same truths and the same observations, trial for trial; pd.concat with a key pairs them. Here the two inversions above, the loose prior and the tight one, are compared on the honest world of the first:

In [43]: both = pd.concat({"loose": trials(problem, post, 400, 0, W),
   ....:                   "tight": trials(problem, post_tight, 400, 0, W)}, names=["inversion"])
   ....: 

In [44]: performance(both).droplevel("block")[["bias", "std", "posterior_std", "coverage"]].round(3)
Out[44]:

          2024-03-01 -0.007  0.201          0.205     0.948

In a world drawn from the loose prior, the tight inversion has no bias on average but claims a third of the spread it has, so its 95% interval holds the truth less than half the time: the prior it trusts is not the one the world drew from.

A world on other state labels#

The two inversions above share a state. Two that do not (a gridded and a basin inversion, a monthly and an annual one, the whole state and the part the solve was restricted to) are compared on totals that both add up to: each state has its own aggregation to the same rows. In the loop that is W on the estimate and another W on the truth; recovery takes the second as W_world, and the error of each total, \(W \hat{x} - W_w x\), keeps its closed form.

The question a year of data raises: does an annual inversion, fed the observations of a seasonal flux, recover the mean flux, or does it alias the season through a footprint that is stronger in some months? The annual inversion has one flux per cell, and its forward operator is the monthly one with each cell’s three columns added. The totals are each cell’s flux: the identity on the annual state, the mean over the months on the monthly one.

In [45]: annual_space = fips.Space({"flux": cells})

In [46]: per_cell = np.kron(np.ones((3, 1)), np.eye(4))  # a monthly element -> its annual cell

In [47]: annual = fips.Problem(
   ....:     prior=fips.Vector(np.ones(4), annual_space, name="prior"),
   ....:     prior_cov=fips.Exponential("x", 1.0).with_std(0.5),
   ....:     obs=fips.Vector(np.zeros(24), obs_space, name="conc"),
   ....:     obs_cov=fips.Independent().with_std(0.3),
   ....:     forward=fips.Operator((weights * sees_month) @ per_cell, obs_space, annual_space),
   ....: )
   ....: 

In [48]: post_annual = fips.solve(annual)

In [49]: W_annual = fips.aggregate.sum(annual_space, by="x")  # each cell's annual flux

In [50]: W_monthly = fips.aggregate.mean(prior.space, by="x")  # the mean over the months

A truth of 1.5 times the prior with a season (one month at 1.3 times its mean, the other two at 0.85) and no noise, against the same truth recovered by the monthly inversion and averaged over the months:

In [51]: def aliasing(season):
   ....:     """Return the bias of each cell's flux from the annual and the monthly inversion."""
   ....:     truth = fips.Vector(1.5 * prior.values * np.repeat(season, 4), prior.space)
   ....:     annual_bias = post_annual.recovery(problem, truth=truth, W=W_annual, W_world=W_monthly)["bias"]
   ....:     monthly_bias = post.recovery(truth=truth, W=W_monthly)["bias"]
   ....:     return pd.DataFrame({"annual": annual_bias, "monthly, averaged": monthly_bias}).droplevel("block")
   ....: 

In [52]: pd.concat({"flat": aliasing([1.0, 1.0, 1.0]), "first month high": aliasing([1.3, 0.85, 0.85])}, axis=1).round(3)
Out[52]: 
      flat                   first month high

With a flat truth both inversions are nearly unbiased. With a season, the annual inversion’s biases are several times its spread while the monthly inversion’s stay small: the season is aliased, because the footprint weights differ between the months and the annual state cannot tell a strong month from a strong cell. The honest world of the monthly problem says the same about the annual inversion’s claimed spread:

Out[53]:

The annual inversion’s prior has no room for the month-to-month variation the world draws, so its intervals are too narrow. The loop takes a world on other labels the same way: the truth through W_world and the estimate through W, and it is the check that recovery is right here too.

Reading the table#

Two questions a real experiment asks are read off the table with pandas. Where does the real retrieval fall among worlds consistent with the prior? Run the honest world and find the quantile of the real estimate in the estimate column of each total: a real number that almost no honest world reaches says the truth was not within the prior’s spread. What truth is consistent with what was retrieved? Regress the truth on the estimate over the trials and read the fit at the real number; the quantiles of the residuals are its interval. For the tight inversion the slope is above 1, because its estimates move less than the truth does, and the fit undoes that shrinkage:

In [54]: tight = both.xs("tight", level="inversion")

In [55]: X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(tight)), tight["estimate"]])

In [56]: beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, tight["truth"], rcond=None)

In [57]: residual = tight["truth"] - X @ beta

In [58]: np.round(beta, 3), np.round(np.percentile(residual, [16, 84]), 3)
Out[58]: (array([-1.556,  1.389]), array([-0.327,  0.323]))

With two inversions paired on the same worlds (a gridded and a basin one, say), the same regression on both estimates reads the truth off the retrieved pair.

Randomness#

Every draw takes rng: a seed, a numpy.random.Generator, or None for fresh entropy, through numpy.random.default_rng(), as SciPy’s rng argument does. One generator passed to every call of a loop gives independent trials; a seed gives the same draws again for the same covariance forms (a Kronecker covariance on part of its grid draws on the whole grid, so changing a model’s form changes the draws). For trials in parallel, spawn one generator per worker, np.random.SeedSequence(seed).spawn(k), and keep each worker’s trials in the table with a worker key, so the run is reproducible whatever the scheduling.
