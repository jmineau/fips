Bounds on the state#

Some quantities cannot leave a range: a rate cannot be negative, a fraction stays between 0 and 1. The Gaussian posterior of a linear problem does not know this, and where the prior is near a bound and the observations see little, its estimate crosses it. fips.solve(problem, "bounded", lower={"flux": 0.0}) solves the same problem with the state held to its bounds (here, a block named flux at or above zero). The estimate is the mode: the analytic estimate moved onto the bounds along the posterior’s correlations. The spread comes from draws of the analytic posterior truncated to the bounds, which the solve keeps.

When to use it#

Use "bounded" when each element’s estimate must respect its bounds (a map with no negative values) and the Gaussian prior cut at the bounds is the prior you mean. The problem is the linear one: a forward operator, a Gaussian prior and Gaussian observation errors.

Another method is better when:

  • you want an aggregate (a sum or a mean over many elements): the analytic posterior’s aggregate is often close to Gaussian, its estimate is unbiased, and it costs least (fips.solve(problem); Keeping a quantity positive);

  • the uncertainty is a factor of a reference, or additive away from zero: solve for a transform of the state that is always positive (LogNormalForward, SoftplusForward; Values that stay positive);

  • the forward map is a model, not a matrix: "bounded" refuses it. Write the model on a state that cannot leave the bounds (the logarithm of a positive value) and solve with "gauss_newton" (A nonlinear forward model).

An example#

As an example, a flux inversion: twenty cells along a line, a prior flux of 0.2 in each with correlated errors, and two sites that see the cells, each with a bias of its own. The bias block must stay free.

In [1]: import numpy as np

In [2]: import pandas as pd

In [3]: import fips

In [4]: rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

In [5]: n, m = 20, 24

In [6]: cells = pd.Index(np.arange(n) + 0.5, name="x")  # km along a line

In [7]: sites = pd.Index(["north", "south"], name="site")

In [8]: prior = fips.Vector.from_blocks(
   ...:     {"flux": pd.Series(0.2, index=cells), "bias": pd.Series(0.0, index=sites)},
   ...:     name="prior",
   ...: )
   ...: 

In [9]: obs_labels = pd.MultiIndex.from_arrays(
   ...:     [np.repeat(sites, m // 2), np.tile(np.arange(m // 2), 2)], names=["site", "hour"]
   ...: )
   ...: 

In [10]: obs_space = fips.Space({"conc": obs_labels})

In [11]: footprint = rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0, (m, n))

In [12]: footprint *= rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0, (m, n)) < 0.3

In [13]: south = (obs_labels.get_level_values("site") == "south").astype(int)

In [14]: H = np.hstack([footprint, np.eye(2)[south]])  # each site sees its own bias

In [15]: forward = fips.Operator(H, obs_space, prior.space)

The data come from a truth that is mostly near zero:

In [16]: flux = np.exp(rng.normal(np.log(0.15), 1.0, n))

In [17]: truth = fips.Vector(np.r_[flux, -0.6, 0.4], prior.space)

In [18]: obs = fips.Vector(H @ truth.values + rng.normal(0.0, 0.2, m), obs_space)

In [19]: problem = fips.Problem(
   ....:     prior=prior,
   ....:     prior_cov=fips.Covariance.block_diag(
   ....:         flux=fips.Exponential("x", 3.0).with_std(0.4),
   ....:         bias=fips.Independent().with_std(0.5),
   ....:     ),
   ....:     obs=obs,
   ....:     obs_cov=fips.Independent().with_std(0.2),
   ....:     forward=forward,
   ....: )
   ....:

The analytic estimate and the bounded one, at the cells on a bound and their neighbors:

In [20]: analytic = fips.solve(problem)

In [21]: post = fips.solve(problem, "bounded", lower={"flux": 0.0})

In [22]: side = pd.DataFrame(
   ....:     {
   ....:         "truth": truth.to_series(),
   ....:         "analytic": analytic.estimate.to_series(),
   ....:         "bounded": post.estimate.to_series(),
   ....:         "at_bound": post.at_bound,
   ....:     }
   ....: )
   ....: 

In [23]: near = post.at_bound.to_numpy()

In [24]: near = near | np.roll(near, 1) | np.roll(near, -1)  # on a bound, or next to one

In [25]: side[near].round(3)
Out[25]: 
                 truth  analytic  bounded  at_bound
block x    site                                    
flux  11.5 nan   0.008     0.008    0.046     False
      12.5 nan   0.088    -0.058    0.000      True
      13.5 nan   0.114    -0.012    0.000      True
      14.5 nan   0.095     0.022    0.025     False

The cells the analytic estimate puts below zero sit at zero, and their neighbors move by what the posterior’s correlations say. Clipping (setting the negative values to zero and nothing else) would always raise the total; moving along the correlations can lower it too. The bias block has no bound and keeps its negative north bias:

In [26]: side.loc["bias"].round(3)
Out[26]: 
           truth  analytic  bounded  at_bound
x   site                                     
NaN north   -0.6    -0.605   -0.617     False
    south    0.4     0.410    0.381     False

The total of the flux, with its 95% interval. With the bounds, the interval is the quantiles of the draws. It is narrower, since the bounds rule out the negative totals the Gaussian allowed, and higher: where the data see little, the draws are those of the prior cut at zero, whose mean is above the prior mean. Here it misses the true total, which the analytic interval holds.

In [27]: W = fips.Operator(
   ....:     np.r_[np.ones(n), 0.0, 0.0][None, :],
   ....:     fips.Space({"total": pd.Index(["flux"], name="what")}),
   ....:     prior.space,
   ....: )
   ....: 

In [28]: (W @ truth).values.round(3)
Out[28]: array([3.452])

In [29]: pd.concat({"analytic": analytic.uncertainty(W), "bounded": post.uncertainty(W)}).round(3)
Out[29]: 
                     prior_std  posterior_std  reduction  lower  upper
         block what                                                   
analytic total flux      4.065          0.952      0.766  1.838  5.568
bounded  total flux      4.065          0.679      0.833  3.894  6.569

The mode is not the mean. The draws spread on the side of the bound away from the mode, so their mean is above it. A question about the distribution (the mean of a total, the chance it is below a value) is read off new draws, sample():

In [30]: draws = post.sample(2000, rng=1, W=W)

In [31]: (W @ post.estimate).values.round(3), round(float(draws.iloc[0].mean()), 3)
Out[31]: (array([3.863]), 5.153)

In [32]: round(float((draws.iloc[0] < 3.0).mean()), 3)
Out[32]: 0.001

What the posterior reports#

A bounded posterior is a sampled one: the members table lists what it gives.

  • estimate is the mode, and at_bound says which elements sit on a bound there: the bounds decide those, and the observations the others.

  • variances, cov() (of the state or of aggregates), mean and the lower and upper of uncertainty() and trials() are those of the kept draws: draws of them, made from seed the first time one is needed, so a solve is reproducible. The interval is the draws’ quantiles, not the mode plus or minus a multiple of the standard deviation. An element on its bound still has spread, on one side of the bound.

  • sample() makes new draws, for any function of the state.

  • The gain, the averaging kernel, DOFS and the information content are those at the mode: the analytic posterior with the elements on a bound held there. An element on a bound has no DOFS: the bound decides it, not the data.

  • chi2 and the misfits are those of the mode. The degrees of freedom and p-values of chi2_tests() are then approximate.

  • with_gamma() and with_data() find the mode again on the kept factors, without factoring again; their draws are made when first needed.

  • recovery() and the data worth raise: they need an estimate linear in the data, and the mode is not. trials() measures what recovery would (Synthetic experiments). log_evidence raises too; the evidence of the problem without bounds is fips.solve(problem).log_evidence.

With no finite bound, the solve returns the analytic posterior itself, a Gaussian one.

Options#

Each is a keyword of fips.solve(problem, "bounded", ...) (BoundedOptions).

Option

Default

What it does

lower, upper

None

The bounds: lower <= x <= upper. A number, a Vector, or a dict by block (below). None is no bound.

distribution

"truncated"

What the draws are draws of: the posterior truncated to the bounds, or "rectified", the bounded estimate with the prior mean and the data drawn again (below).

draws

2000

The number of draws kept for the variances, the covariances, the mean and the intervals. The Monte Carlo error of a standard deviation falls as one over its square root.

burn_in

20

The draws of the truncated chain made and left out before those kept. Rectified draws are not a chain, and leave none out.

seed

0

The seed of the kept draws.

gamma

1.0

The regularization factor: the observation covariance is divided by it, as for the analytic method.

Giving the bounds#

The bounds go to the solve, not to the Problem: the problem is the Gaussian question, and the bounds are how this method answers it. Each of lower and upper is one of:

  • a number, for every state element. On a state of several blocks that bounds the bias block too, which is rarely meant, and the solve warns;

  • a Vector with exactly the state’s labels, one bound per element, -inf (or inf for upper) where an element has none;

  • a dict by block, such as {"flux": 0.0}: each value a number or a Vector on that block’s labels. Blocks it does not name are free.

Truncated or rectified draws#

Truncated draws (the default) are draws of the analytic posterior truncated to the bounds: the Bayesian posterior of the Gaussian prior cut at the bounds. A truncated draw is never exactly on a bound.

Rectified draws (distribution="rectified") are the bounded estimate itself, with the inputs drawn again: each is the bounded problem solved for one draw of the prior mean from \(N(x_0, S_0)\) and of the observations from \(N(z, S_z)\). An element that the draw puts outside its bounds is exactly on its bound, as at the mode. They are the distribution of the bounded estimate over inputs as likely as these. They are not the truncated posterior, and not the Bayesian posterior of any prior fips writes down.

The estimate, at_bound, the gain and DOFS are the same either way; the draws differ, and with them the variances, the mean, the intervals and the aggregates. Where the data see little, the truncated draws’ mean sits above the prior mean at a lower bound, and the rectified draws’ less far above:

In [33]: rectified = fips.solve(problem, "bounded", lower={"flux": 0.0}, distribution="rectified")

In [34]: first = post.at_bound[post.at_bound].index[0]  # the first cell on its bound

In [35]: on_bound = {"truncated": post, "rectified": rectified}

In [36]: {name: float((p.sample(2000, rng=1).loc[first] == 0).mean()) for name, p in on_bound.items()}
Out[36]: {'truncated': 0.0, 'rectified': 0.499}

In [37]: pd.concat({"truncated": post.uncertainty(W), "rectified": rectified.uncertainty(W)}).round(3)
Out[37]: 
                      prior_std  posterior_std  reduction  lower  upper
          block what                                                   
truncated total flux      4.065          0.679      0.833  3.894  6.569
rectified total flux      4.065          0.660      0.838  3.140  5.737

About half the rectified draws hold that cell at exactly zero, and none of the truncated ones do. The rectified total sits lower, and here its interval holds the true total. For an aggregate, read the analytic posterior; rectified draws suit the elements of a map near a bound, where the truth may be exactly on it. Comparing ways to keep a quantity positive compares the methods on one problem.

Warnings and checks#

fips logs its warnings through Python’s logging, which is silent until you configure it (Repeated inversions, logging and saving). The solve logs a warning when:

  • a number bounds a state of several blocks. It bounds every block, a bias block too. Give a dict to bound only some: lower={"flux": 0.0}.

  • the prior is wide against its bounds: a prior standard deviation over half the distance to a bound, for more than a tenth of a block’s bounded elements. Where the data see little, the draws’ mean, intervals and aggregates are those of the prior as the draws treat it, cut at the bounds (or, for rectified draws, set to them), and so above the prior mean at a lower bound. The warning names the block and says by how much its aggregate sits above the prior’s, as a share of its distance from the bounds. The estimate is not affected. For an aggregate, read the analytic posterior or use LogNormalForward.

  • the kept draws are too few: a standard deviation from them is uncertain by more than 10%. It says how many draws would do; raise draws. Truncated draws mix slowest at an element whose analytic estimate is one to a few standard deviations outside its bound; rectified draws are too few at an element on its bound in nearly every draw.

It raises:

  • TypeError if the problem’s forward is a model, or a bound is not a number, a Vector, a dict or None;

  • ValueError if lower is not below upper at some element (an element held at one value is known, not bounded), a bound is NaN or one no value can satisfy (a lower of inf), a Vector’s labels are not the state’s (or its block’s), or a dict names a block the state does not have;

  • numpy.linalg.LinAlgError if elements on a bound cannot be held there: an element whose posterior variance is zero, or elements whose posterior errors are tied together. Check the bounds of the elements the prior or the observations fix.

A member a sampled posterior cannot give raises UnavailableError (the table).

Cost#

The mode costs little more than the analytic solve: the factors, plus a column of the posterior covariance \(\hat{S}\) for each element that ends on a bound.

The draws cost more, and are made only when a spread is first asked for. A truncated draw follows a path that bounces off each bound it meets, and each bounce costs a step of the size of the state, so the cost grows with the number of elements near a bound: for a thousand elements mostly near zero, the default 2000 draws take tens of seconds, and a state far from its bounds bounces little. A rectified draw costs an active set, as the mode does. Both keep the columns of \(\hat{S}\) at every element a draw holds on a bound, up to all of them (\(n \times n\)). The cost is proportional to draws; trials() draws in every trial, so keep draws small there.

How it works#

The posterior. The prior with the bounds is the Gaussian prior set to zero outside them. The likelihood is unchanged, so the posterior is the analytic posterior \(N(\hat{x}, \hat{S})\) truncated to the box \(l \le x \le u\). It is not Gaussian, and its mean is not its mode.

The mode is the point of the box where the analytic cost is least. The cost is quadratic, so that is the point of the box nearest \(\hat{x}\) in the metric of the posterior:

\[x^\ast = \arg\min_{l \le x \le u} (x - \hat{x})^T \hat{S}^{-1} (x - \hat{x}).\]

With \(a\) the set of elements held on a bound \(b_a\), the answer is \(x^\ast = \hat{x} + \hat{S}_{:a} \lambda_a\), with \(\lambda_a = \hat{S}_{aa}^{-1} (b_a - \hat{x}_a)\): the held elements go to their bounds, and the others move by what the posterior says they would if those elements were known to be there. An active set finds \(a\), exchanging at each step every element to hold or release (Nocedal and Wright, 2006; Lawson and Hanson, 1974, for nonnegative least squares), and needs \(\hat{S}\) only at the columns of \(a\), from the analytic factors. The solve checks the optimality conditions of the answer before it returns it.

The diagnostics at the mode are those of the analytic posterior with the elements of \(a\) held at their bounds. With \(C = \hat{S}_{aa}\),

\[K_a = K - \hat{S}_{:a} C^{-1} K_{a:}, \qquad A_a = K_a H = A - \hat{S}_{:a} C^{-1} A_{a:},\]

and an element on a bound has a row of zeros in both.

Truncated draws are made by exact Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (Pakman and Paninski, 2014). Each draw starts from the last, \(x_0\), with a velocity \(v \sim N(0, \hat{S})\), and follows

\[x(t) = \hat{x} + (x_0 - \hat{x}) \cos t + v \sin t\]

for a quarter period, \(t = \pi/2\). Each element’s time to reach its bound has a closed form; at the first bound reached, the velocity reflects and the path goes on. With no bound in the way, \(x(\pi/2) = \hat{x} + v\) is a draw independent of the start. There is no step size and no rejection. The draws are correlated only through the bounces: an element away from its bounds has about one independent draw per draw (a standard deviation from 2000 is good to about 1.5%), while one whose analytic estimate is one to four standard deviations outside its bound piles against it and mixes an order of magnitude slower.

Rectified draws use one fact: the analytic estimate of the problem with a drawn prior mean and drawn observations is an exact draw \(x_s\) of \(N(\hat{x}, \hat{S})\), and that problem has the same \(\hat{S}\). Its bounded estimate is therefore \(x_s\) moved onto the bounds by the same active set as the mode, with \(x_s\) in place of \(\hat{x}\). So draw k of rectified.sample(N, rng=5) is draw k of fips.solve(problem).sample(N, rng=5), unchanged where no bound binds. The draws are independent. Miller, Michalak and Levi (2014, section 3.3.1) propose realizations this way and then accept or reject each by a Metropolis-Hastings test, which fips does not; Bardsley and Fox (2012) solve a nonnegativity-constrained problem for each sample; Everink, Dong and Andersen (2023) call the resulting distribution a regularized Gaussian.
