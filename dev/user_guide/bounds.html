Bounds on the state#

A flux of methane cannot be negative, yet the Gaussian posterior of a flux inversion often is in cells where the prior is small and the observations see little. fips.solve(problem, "bounded", lower={"flux": 0.0}) holds the state to bounds. The rule: the bounded estimate is the analytic one moved onto the bounds along the posterior’s correlations. An element the analytic estimate puts below its bound goes to the bound, and its neighbors move by what the posterior says they would if that element were known to be at its bound. A neighbor the move would push out is held at its bound too. Clipping (setting the negative cells to zero and nothing else) is not this: it always raises the total, while moving along the correlations can lower it as well.

What the bounded posterior is#

The prior with the bounds is the Gaussian prior set to zero outside them (whose mean is above the Gaussian’s: Choosing a method shows how far), so the posterior with the bounds is the analytic posterior \(N(\hat{x}, \hat{S})\) truncated to the box \(l \le x \le u\). It is not Gaussian, and its mean is not its mode:

  • the estimate is the mode, the point of the box where the analytic cost is least, which is the point of the box nearest \(\hat{x}\) in the metric of \(\hat{S}\). fips finds it with an active set that needs only the columns of \(\hat{S}\) at the elements on a bound, from the analytic factors, and checks the optimality conditions of the answer before it returns it. post.mean raises: the mode is not the mean;

  • the uncertainty comes from draws of the truncated posterior, by exact Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (Pakman and Paninski, 2014). An element on its bound still has spread, on one side of the bound.

The bounds are lower and upper: a number for every element, a Vector with an element’s own bound (-inf or inf for none), or a dict by block. {"flux": 0.0} bounds the fluxes and leaves the other blocks free, as a bias block must be. Bounds go to the solve, not to the Problem: the problem is the Gaussian question, and the bounds are how the method answers it.

A small problem#

Twenty cells along a line, with a prior of 0.2 and correlated errors, seen from two sites, each with a bias of its own; the data come from a truth drawn lognormal, most of it near zero:

In [2]: import pandas as pd

In [3]: import fips

In [4]: rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

In [7]: sites = pd.Index(["north", "south"], name="site")

   ...: )

   ...:     [np.repeat(sites, n_obs // 2), np.tile(np.arange(n_obs // 2), 2)],
   ...:     names=["site", "hour"],
   ...: )
   ...: 

In [10]: obs_space = fips.Space({"ch4": obs_labels})

In [11]: footprint = rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0, (n_obs, n_cells))

In [12]: footprint *= rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0, (n_obs, n_cells)) < 0.3

In [13]: south = (obs_labels.get_level_values("site") == "south").astype(int)

In [14]: H = np.hstack([footprint, np.eye(2)[south]])  # each site sees its own bias

In [15]: forward = fips.Operator(H, obs_space, prior.space)

In [16]: def build(obs):
   ....:     """The problem of the observations `obs`."""
   ....:     return fips.Problem(
   ....:         prior=prior,
   ....:         prior_cov=fips.Covariance.block_diag(
   ....:             flux=fips.Exponential("x", 3.0).with_std(0.4),
   ....:             bias=fips.Independent().with_std(0.5),
   ....:         ),
   ....:         obs=obs,
   ....:         obs_cov=fips.Independent().with_std(0.2),
   ....:         forward=forward,
   ....:     )
   ....: 

In [17]: flux = np.exp(rng.normal(np.log(0.15), 1.0, n_cells))

In [18]: truth = fips.Vector(np.r_[flux, -0.6, 0.4], prior.space)

In [19]: obs = fips.Vector(H @ truth.values + rng.normal(0.0, 0.2, n_obs), obs_space)

In [20]: problem = build(obs)

The analytic posterior and the bounded one, side by side. Two cells go below zero in the analytic estimate; with the bounds they sit at zero, their neighbors move a little, and the bias block, unbounded, keeps its negative north bias:

at_bound says which elements sit on a bound at the mode: the elements the bounds decide, where the observations decide the others.

What the posterior says#

The spread comes from draws. variances, cov() (of the state or of totals) and uncertainty() are those of the draws the solve keeps: draws=2000 of them by default, made at the first need from seed=0, so a solve is reproducible. They are Monte Carlo estimates (a standard deviation from 2000 draws is good to about 2%). The total’s uncertainty is smaller with the bounds: they rule out the negative totals the Gaussian allowed.

In [28]: W = fips.Operator(
   ....:     np.r_[np.ones(n_cells), 0.0, 0.0][None, :],
   ....:     fips.Space({"total": pd.Index(["flux"], name="what")}),
   ....:     prior.space,
   ....: )
   ....: 

In [29]: pd.concat({"analytic": analytic.uncertainty(W), "bounded": post.uncertainty(W)}).round(3)
Out[29]: 
                     prior_std  posterior_std  reduction
         block what                                     
analytic total flux      4.065          0.952      0.766
bounded  total flux      4.065          0.679      0.833

The mode is not the mean. The estimate is the mode; the draws are spread on the side of the bound away from it, so their mean is above it for a lower bound. A question about the distribution (a total’s mean, the chance it is below a value) is read off sample(), new draws of the truncated posterior:

In [30]: draws = post.sample(2000, rng=1, W=W)

In [31]: (W @ post.estimate).values.round(3), round(float(draws.iloc[0].mean()), 3)
Out[31]: (array([3.863]), 5.153)

In [32]: round(float((draws.iloc[0] < 3.0).mean()), 3)
Out[32]: 0.001

The gain, DOFS and the averaging kernel are those at the mode: the analytic posterior with the elements on a bound held there. An element on a bound has no DOFS, since the bound, not the data, decides it. The chi-squared and the misfits are those of the mode (the chi-squared tests’ degrees of freedom and p-values are then approximate):

In [33]: post.dofs_per_element.to_series()[post.at_bound].round(3)
Out[33]: 
block  x     site
flux   12.5  nan     0.0
       13.5  nan     0.0
Name: dofs_per_element, dtype: float64

In [34]: round(analytic.dofs, 3), round(post.dofs, 3)
Out[34]: (13.634, 12.633)

with_gamma() and with_data() find the mode again on the kept factors, without solving again, and their draws are made when first needed. recovery() and the data worth refuse a bounded posterior, whose estimate is not linear in the data: trials() measures it instead (below).

What it costs#

The mode costs little more than the analytic solve: the active set needs the columns of \(\hat{S}\) at the elements on a bound, a product with the factors for each, and a solve with \(\hat{S}\) at those elements at each of its steps. The draws cost more. Each draw follows a path that bounces off every bound it reaches, each bounce a step of the size of the state, and a path reaches about as many bounds as there are elements near one. A state of a thousand elements, most of them near zero, took about 20 ms a draw (40 s for 2000), and one of three thousand about 0.1 s a draw, on one machine; a state far from its bounds bounces little and costs little. The draws are made only when a spread is asked for, and a smaller draws (fips.solve(problem, "bounded", lower=0.0, draws=500)) costs proportionally less, with a larger Monte Carlo error.

trials() asks each trial’s posterior for its spread, so a bounded posterior draws in every trial: keep draws small there.

In a world with a nonnegative truth#

Does holding the fluxes at or above zero give a better answer? It depends on what is asked, and trials() (see Synthetic experiments) measures it: the same data, trial for trial (the same seed), solved with and without the bounds, in a world whose truth is lognormal, most of it near zero, which neither posterior assumes. The rows of the aggregation are each cell and the total:

In [35]: def world(rng):
   ....:     """A lognormal flux, a Gaussian bias, and the observations they give."""
   ....:     x = np.r_[np.exp(rng.normal(np.log(0.15), 1.0, n_cells)), rng.normal(0.0, 0.5, 2)]
   ....:     return fips.Vector(x, prior.space), fips.Vector(
   ....:         H @ x + rng.normal(0.0, 0.2, n_obs), obs_space
   ....:     )
   ....: 

In [36]: rows = fips.Space({"cell": cells, "total": pd.Index(["flux"], name="what")})

In [37]: each_and_total = np.vstack([np.eye(n_cells, n_cells + 2), np.r_[np.ones(n_cells), 0.0, 0.0]])

In [38]: W_rows = fips.Operator(each_and_total, rows, prior.space)

In [39]: small = fips.solve(problem, "bounded", lower={"flux": 0.0}, draws=300)

In [40]: tables = {
   ....:     "analytic": analytic.trials(world, 100, rng=7, W=W_rows),
   ....:     "bounded": small.trials(world, 100, rng=7, W=W_rows),
   ....: }
   ....: 

In [41]: def scores(table):
   ....:     """The cells' RMSE, and the total's bias, spread, claimed spread and 95% coverage."""
   ....:     error = table["estimate"] - table["truth"]
   ....:     cell = error.xs("cell", level="block")
   ....:     total = table.xs("total", level="block")
   ....:     total_error = total["estimate"] - total["truth"]
   ....:     covered = total_error.abs() <= 1.96 * total["posterior_std"]
   ....:     return pd.Series(
   ....:         {
   ....:             "cell RMSE": float(np.sqrt((cell**2).mean())),
   ....:             "total bias": total_error.mean(),
   ....:             "total spread": total_error.std(),
   ....:             "claimed spread": total["posterior_std"].mean(),
   ....:             "total coverage": covered.mean(),
   ....:         }
   ....:     )
   ....: 

In [42]: pd.DataFrame({name: scores(table) for name, table in tables.items()}).round(3)
Out[42]: 
                analytic  bounded
cell RMSE          0.211    0.193
total bias         0.109    0.386
total spread       1.003    0.870
claimed spread     0.952    0.748
total coverage     0.900    0.880

With 100 trials the bias is known to about a tenth of the spread, and a coverage near 0.9 to about 0.03. In this world the bounds make the map better (a smaller cell RMSE) and move the total: up, by a few Monte Carlo standard errors of the bias, with a smaller spread and a smaller claimed spread (the two coverages are within their Monte Carlo error of each other). That is the pattern to expect: bounds mostly improve maps and can move totals, and since the estimate is the mode of a skewed posterior, a total read from it is not the posterior’s mean total (above, the draws’). Which matters depends on the question. For a basin total, read the analytic posterior: the draws’ total is that of the truncated prior wherever the data see little, above an inventory that is the mean, and Choosing a method measures it far above in a Salt Lake Valley-shaped problem. The world here is a toy: the trials of an inversion’s own problem, at its own size, say what its bounds do.
