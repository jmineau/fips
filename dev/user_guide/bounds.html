Bounds on the state#

A flux of methane cannot be negative, yet the Gaussian posterior of a flux inversion often is in cells where the prior is small and the observations see little. fips.solve(problem, "bounded", lower={"flux": 0.0}) holds the state to bounds. The rule: the bounded estimate is the analytic one moved onto the bounds along the posterior’s correlations. An element the analytic estimate puts below its bound goes to the bound, and its neighbors move by what the posterior says they would if that element were known to be at its bound. A neighbor the move would push out is held at its bound too. Clipping (setting the negative cells to zero and nothing else) is not this: it always raises the total, while moving along the correlations can lower it as well.

What the bounded posterior is#

The prior with the bounds is the Gaussian prior set to zero outside them (whose mean is above the Gaussian’s: Choosing a method shows how far), so the posterior with the bounds is the analytic posterior \(N(\hat{x}, \hat{S})\) truncated to the box \(l \le x \le u\). It is not Gaussian, and its mean is not its mode:

  • the estimate is the mode, the point of the box where the analytic cost is least, which is the point of the box nearest \(\hat{x}\) in the metric of \(\hat{S}\). fips finds it with an active set that needs only the columns of \(\hat{S}\) at the elements on a bound, from the analytic factors, and checks the optimality conditions of the answer before it returns it. The mode is not the mean: post.mean is the mean of the draws;

  • the uncertainty comes from draws of the truncated posterior, by exact Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (Pakman and Paninski, 2014). An element on its bound still has spread, on one side of the bound. The intervals of uncertainty() and trials() (lower and upper) are the draws’ quantiles, not the mode plus or minus a multiple of the standard deviation.

Where the prior is wide against its bounds (a standard deviation over half the distance to a bound, for more than a tenth of a block’s bounded elements) the solve logs one warning naming the block, how many of its elements are wide, and by how much the cut prior’s total sits above the prior’s (below it, at an upper bound), each element taken alone, as a percentage of the total’s distance from the bounds: of the prior’s total, for a bound of zero. That is how far the draws’ totals sit from the prior’s where the data constrain little; the estimate is not affected. With rectified draws (below) it gives the smaller shift of the prior set to its bounds.

The bounds are lower and upper: a number for every element, a Vector with an element’s own bound (-inf or inf for none), or a dict by block. {"flux": 0.0} bounds the fluxes and leaves the other blocks free, as a bias block must be. Bounds go to the solve, not to the Problem: the problem is the Gaussian question, and the bounds are how the method answers it.

What the posterior says#

The spread comes from draws. variances, cov() (of the state or of totals) and uncertainty() are those of the draws the solve keeps: draws=2000 of them by default, made at the first need from seed=0, so a solve is reproducible. They are Monte Carlo estimates from a chain, each draw exact but started from the last: from 2000 draws a standard deviation is good to about 1.5% for an element away from its bounds, and to about 12% for one whose analytic estimate is one to four standard deviations outside its bound, whose draws pile against it and mix slowly (more draws make it better as one over their square root; the solve warns when a standard deviation is uncertain by more than 10%, with the number of draws that would do). The total’s uncertainty is smaller with the bounds: they rule out the negative totals the Gaussian allowed.

In [28]: W = fips.Operator(
   ....:     np.r_[np.ones(n_cells), 0.0, 0.0][None, :],
   ....:     fips.Space({"total": pd.Index(["flux"], name="what")}),
   ....:     prior.space,
   ....: )
   ....:

The mode is not the mean. The estimate is the mode; the draws are spread on the side of the bound away from it, so their mean is above it for a lower bound. A question about the distribution (a total’s mean, the chance it is below a value) is read off sample(), new draws of the truncated posterior:

In [30]: draws = post.sample(2000, rng=1, W=W)

In [31]: (W @ post.estimate).values.round(3), round(float(draws.iloc[0].mean()), 3)
Out[31]: (array([3.863]), 5.153)

In [32]: round(float((draws.iloc[0] < 3.0).mean()), 3)
Out[32]: 0.001

The gain, DOFS and the averaging kernel are those at the mode: the analytic posterior with the elements on a bound held there. An element on a bound has no DOFS, since the bound, not the data, decides it. The chi-squared and the misfits are those of the mode (the chi-squared tests’ degrees of freedom and p-values are then approximate):

In [33]: post.dofs_per_element.to_series()[post.at_bound].round(3)
Out[33]:

flux   12.5  nan     0.0
       13.5  nan     0.0

In [34]: round(analytic.dofs, 3), round(post.dofs, 3)
Out[34]: (13.634, 12.633)

with_gamma() and with_data() find the mode again on the kept factors, without solving again, and their draws are made when first needed. A bounded posterior is a sampled one: the members it cannot give (recovery() and the data worth among them, since its estimate is not linear in the data) are in the table, and trials() measures what recovery would (below).

Rectified draws#

Rectified draws are the bounded estimate itself, with the prior mean and the data redrawn (distribution="rectified"). Each draw is the bounded problem solved again for one draw of the prior mean from \(N(x_0, S_0)\) and of the observations from \(N(z, S_z)\), so an element that draw puts outside its bounds is exactly on its bound, as at the mode. The default draws, of the truncated posterior, are never exactly on a bound. The estimate, the active set, the gain and DOFS are the same either way; only the draws differ, and with them the variances, post.mean, the intervals and totals.

A cell on its bound at the mode, in the small problem above:

What the rectified draws are, and are not. They are the distribution of the bounded estimate when the prior mean and the data are redrawn from their Gaussians: what the inversion would give for other inputs as likely as these. They are not, in general, the Bayesian posterior of any prior fips writes down, and they are not the truncated posterior. For one element whose prior mean is one standard deviation above a bound of zero, with no data, the rectified mean is 1.083 times the prior mean (the truncated one is 1.288 times), and 16% of the draws are exactly zero. The construction is old: Miller, Michalak and Levi (2014, section 3.3.1) propose realizations this way (perturbed inputs, then the bounded solve) and then accept or reject each by a Metropolis-Hastings test, which fips does not; Bardsley and Fox (2012) solve a nonnegativity-constrained quadratic problem for each sample; Everink, Dong and Andersen (2023) call the resulting distribution a regularized Gaussian.

How often their intervals hold the truth, against the analytic posterior and the truncated draws: 50 cells on a line whose prior standard deviation equals its mean (correlated over 5 cells), 40 observations, 300 trials of data from the problem’s own error (a coverage near 0.9 is known to about 0.02), 1000 draws a trial. The truth is a draw of the Gaussian prior, the same with its negative cells set to zero, or a draw of the prior truncated at zero. With stronger data (a standard deviation a tenth of the prior’s) the totals were 0.97, 0.70, 0.84; 0.98, 0.81, 0.94; and 0.96, 0.95, 0.96. The rectified intervals hold the truth more often than the truncated ones wherever the truth is not a draw of the truncated prior, and as often where it is; the analytic total held it in every world, while the rectified total fell short of 95% where the truth could be negative (0.77 and 0.84) and, with weak data, where it had exact zeros (0.88). For a total, read the analytic posterior. The rectified draws suit the cells of a map near zero: where the truth has exact zeros, 96% of their cells’ intervals held it, against 77% for the truncated draws, which never reach zero.

How they are made: the analytic estimate of the perturbed problem is an exact draw of the analytic posterior \(N(\hat{x}, \hat{S})\) (what sample() of fips.solve(problem) returns), and its bounded estimate is that draw moved onto the bounds along the posterior’s correlations, by the same active set as the mode. So draw k of rectified.sample(n, rng=5) is draw k of fips.solve(problem).sample(n, rng=5), unchanged where no bound binds in it. The draws are independent, not a chain: burn_in does nothing, and the solve warns when a standard deviation from them is uncertain by more than 10%, which happens for an element on its bound in nearly every draw, whose spread comes from the few that are not. With rectified draws, the wide-prior warning gives the shift of the prior set to its bounds, not cut at them (8% of the distance to a bound of zero, against 29%, for a standard deviation equal to that distance).

What it costs#

The mode costs little more than the analytic solve: the active set needs the columns of \(\hat{S}\) at the elements on a bound, a product with the factors for each, and a solve with \(\hat{S}\) at those elements at each of its steps. The draws cost more. Each draw follows a path that bounces off every bound it reaches, each bounce a step of the size of the state, and a path reaches about as many bounds as there are elements near one. A state of a thousand elements, most of them near zero, took about 20 ms a draw (40 s for 2000), and one of three thousand about 0.1 s a draw, on one machine; a state far from its bounds bounces little and costs little. The draws are made only when a spread is asked for, and a smaller draws (fips.solve(problem, "bounded", lower={"flux": 0.0}, draws=500)) costs proportionally less, with a larger Monte Carlo error.

A rectified draw costs an active set, as the mode does: a few steps, each exchanging at once every element to hold or release, and each a solve with \(\hat{S}\) at the elements on a bound. With a correlated prior whose standard deviation equals its mean and weak data, a draw of 400 elements (70 of them on a bound in a typical draw) took 1.7 ms against 4 ms for a truncated one, and a draw of 2000 elements (120 on a bound) 7.6 ms against 14 ms: 15 s for the default 2000 draws, on one machine. Both keep the same columns of \(\hat{S}\), up to all of them (n x n).

trials() asks each trial’s posterior for its spread, so a bounded posterior draws in every trial: keep draws small there.

In a world with a nonnegative truth#

Does holding the fluxes at or above zero give a better answer? It depends on what is asked, and trials() (see Synthetic experiments) measures it: the same data, trial for trial (the same seed), solved with and without the bounds, in a world whose truth is lognormal, most of it near zero, which neither posterior assumes. The rows of the aggregation are each cell and the total:

In [40]: def world(rng):
   ....:     """A lognormal flux, a Gaussian bias, and the observations they give."""
   ....:     x = np.r_[np.exp(rng.normal(np.log(0.15), 1.0, n_cells)), rng.normal(0.0, 0.5, 2)]
   ....:     return fips.Vector(x, prior.space), fips.Vector(
   ....:         H @ x + rng.normal(0.0, 0.2, n_obs), obs_space
   ....:     )
   ....: 

In [41]: rows = fips.Space({"cell": cells, "total": pd.Index(["flux"], name="what")})

In [42]: each_and_total = np.vstack([np.eye(n_cells, n_cells + 2), np.r_[np.ones(n_cells), 0.0, 0.0]])

In [43]: W_rows = fips.Operator(each_and_total, rows, prior.space)

In [44]: small = fips.solve(problem, "bounded", lower={"flux": 0.0}, draws=300)

In [45]: tables = {
   ....:     "analytic": analytic.trials(world, 100, rng=7, W=W_rows),
   ....:     "bounded": small.trials(world, 100, rng=7, W=W_rows),
   ....: }
   ....: 

In [46]: def scores(table):
   ....:     """The cells' RMSE, and the total's bias, spread, claimed spread and 95% coverage."""
   ....:     error = table["estimate"] - table["truth"]
   ....:     cell = error.xs("cell", level="block")
   ....:     total = table.xs("total", level="block")
   ....:     total_error = total["estimate"] - total["truth"]
   ....:     covered = (total["lower"] <= total["truth"]) & (total["truth"] <= total["upper"])
   ....:     return pd.Series(
   ....:         {
   ....:             "cell RMSE": float(np.sqrt((cell**2).mean())),
   ....:             "total bias": total_error.mean(),
   ....:             "total spread": total_error.std(),
   ....:             "claimed spread": total["posterior_std"].mean(),
   ....:             "total coverage": covered.mean(),
   ....:         }
   ....:     )
   ....:

With 100 trials the bias is known to about a tenth of the spread, and a coverage near 0.9 to about 0.03. In this world the bounds make the map better (a smaller cell RMSE) and move the total: up, by a few Monte Carlo standard errors of the bias, with a smaller spread and a smaller claimed spread. The bounded total’s interval, the quantiles of its draws, holds the truth much less often than the analytic one: the draws are of the truncated posterior, whose total is above this world’s wherever the data see little. That is the pattern to expect: bounds mostly improve maps and can move totals, and since the estimate is the mode of a skewed posterior, a total read from it is not the posterior’s mean total (above, the draws’). Which matters depends on the question. For a basin total, read the analytic posterior: the draws’ total is that of the truncated prior wherever the data see little, above an inventory that is the mean, and Choosing a method measures it far above in a Salt Lake Valley-shaped problem. The world here is a toy: the trials of an inversion’s own problem, at its own size, say what its bounds do.
