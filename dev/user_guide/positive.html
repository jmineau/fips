Values that stay positive#

Some quantities cannot be negative (a rate, a concentration, an amount), yet the Gaussian posterior of a problem that estimates them often is negative where the prior is small and the observations see little. One way to keep them positive is to hold the state to bounds (Bounds on the state). The other, on this page: solve for a variable \(u\) that can be anything, and make the value a positive function of it, \(T(u)\). Two forward models do this:

  • fips.LogNormalForward: the value is a factor of a reference, \(r\, e^{u}\);

  • fips.SoftplusForward: the value is \(u\) itself where it is large, and bends to stay above zero near zero.

The state, its prior and its covariance are in \(u\)’s units. model.prior_for gives the prior on \(u\) whose value has the mean and standard deviation you mean. fips.solve(problem, "gauss_newton") finds the mode of \(u\) and draws its posterior (A nonlinear forward model). model.transform maps the estimate to the value at the mode, and the draws to draws of the value.

When to use it#

  • LogNormalForward when the uncertainty is a factor of a reference (“a factor of 2”), the same in relative terms at small and large values. An element whose reference is zero stays zero.

  • SoftplusForward when the uncertainty is additive (“10 ± 2”, whatever the value’s size), and the value is near zero only in some elements, where it bends instead of going below.

  • "bounded" (Bounds on the state) instead, when the Gaussian prior is the one you mean and only its negative part is wrong.

  • The analytic posterior (fips.solve(problem)), when what you need is an aggregate of many elements: it ignores the bound, but its aggregate is often close to Gaussian, and it costs least.

Each puts a different prior on the state; Keeping a quantity positive compares them, and Comparing ways to keep a quantity positive runs them on one problem.

An example#

As an example, a flux inversion: the value is the flux of each of forty cells along a line, and the reference is an emission inventory with three empty cells. Thirty observations each see a few neighboring cells:

In [1]: import numpy as np

In [2]: import pandas as pd

In [3]: import fips

In [4]: rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

In [5]: cells = fips.Space({"flux": pd.RangeIndex(40, name="cell")})

In [6]: observed = fips.Space({"conc": pd.RangeIndex(30, name="obs_id")})

In [7]: footprint = np.zeros((30, 40))

In [8]: for i, start in enumerate(rng.integers(0, 34, 30)):
   ...:     footprint[i, start : start + rng.integers(2, 7)] = rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5)
   ...: 

In [9]: H = fips.Operator(footprint, observed, cells)

In [10]: inventory = pd.Series(rng.lognormal(0.0, 0.8, 40), index=cells.select("flux"))

In [11]: inventory.iloc[[5, 17, 29]] = 0.0

In [12]: model = fips.LogNormalForward(H, inventory)

In [13]: model
Out[13]: LogNormalForward(block: 'flux', rows: Space(conc: 30 x ['obs_id']), cols: Space(flux: 40 x ['cell']))

The state on the flux block is \(u\), the logarithm of the factor that scales each cell’s inventory, and the model is \(F(x) = H\, (r\, e^{u})\). \(H\) is the forward operator on the whole state: with a bias block too, name the transformed block with block="flux", and the bias passes through as it is.

The prior on u#

prior_for() takes the prior mean and standard deviation of each element’s value and returns the mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\) of \(u\) that give exactly those. Here the inventory is the prior mean, with an uncertainty of 100%:

In [14]: mu, sigma = model.prior_for(mean=inventory, std=inventory)

In [15]: pd.DataFrame(
   ....:     {"inventory": inventory, "mu": mu.to_series("flux"), "sigma": sigma.to_series("flux")}
   ....: ).iloc[3:7].round(3)
   ....: 
Out[15]: 
      inventory     mu  sigma
cell                         
3         0.673 -0.347  0.833
4         1.301 -0.347  0.833
5         0.000  0.000  0.000
6         3.549 -0.347  0.833

An uncertainty of 100% is \(\sigma = \sqrt{\log 2} = 0.83\), a factor of 2.3 either way. \(\mu\) is below zero so that the prior mean of the value is the inventory; a prior of \(\mu = 0\) would make the inventory the prior median instead, and the prior’s total higher than the inventory’s (How it works, below). The correlations are those of \(u\): write the covariance with them as for any state, fips.Exponential("cell", 3.0).with_std(sigma).

Solving and reading the value#

The problem, with data made from a truth drawn from that prior:

In [16]: def build(obs, model=model, mu=mu, sigma=sigma):
   ....:     """The problem of the observations `obs`: the state is u, its prior from prior_for."""
   ....:     return fips.Problem(
   ....:         prior=mu,
   ....:         prior_cov=fips.Exponential("cell", 3.0).with_std(sigma),
   ....:         obs=obs,
   ....:         obs_cov=fips.Independent().with_std(0.2),
   ....:         forward=model,
   ....:         offset=10.0,
   ....:     )
   ....: 

In [17]: truth, obs = build(fips.Vector(np.zeros(30), observed)).simulate(rng=1)

In [18]: post = fips.solve(build(obs), "gauss_newton")

In [19]: post.iterations
Out[19]:

The estimate is the mode of \(u\). transform() maps it to the flux at the mode, which is near each cell’s posterior median, not its mean. The same transform maps draws of \(u\) (sample(), one column each) to draws of the flux, and a cell’s interval is the quantiles of its draws:

In [20]: flux = model.transform(post.estimate).to_series("flux")

In [21]: draws = model.transform(post.sample(4000, rng=2))

In [22]: low, high = draws.quantile(0.025, axis=1).to_numpy(), draws.quantile(0.975, axis=1).to_numpy()

In [23]: true_flux = model.transform(truth).to_series("flux")

In [24]: table = pd.DataFrame({"truth": true_flux, "inventory": inventory, "at the mode": flux})

Elements with no reference#

An element whose reference is zero has a value of zero, whatever \(u\) is. Its column of the Jacobian is zero, so no observation moves it. prior_for gives those elements \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma = 0\); any prior there gives the other elements the same posterior.

In [28]: flux.iloc[[5, 17, 29]]
Out[28]: 
cell
5     0.0
17    0.0
29    0.0
Name: estimate, dtype: float64

To let the data put a value there, give the element a reference (a small one stays small, since it grows by the prior’s factor), use SoftplusForward with a prior mean above zero, or use bounds (Bounds on the state).

The softplus model#

fips.SoftplusForward solves for the value in its own units: the value is \(u\) where \(u\) is several scale above zero, and bends to stay positive near zero. scale is in the value’s units and says where the bend is, so it has no default: set it near the prior standard deviation of the elements that could be near zero. A softplus value is never zero, so here the empty cells get a prior mean of a tenth of the smallest inventory:

In [29]: softplus = fips.SoftplusForward(H, scale=0.5)

In [30]: mean = inventory.where(inventory > 0, 0.1 * inventory[inventory > 0].min())

In [31]: mu_s, sigma_s = softplus.prior_for(mean, 0.5 * mean)

In [32]: pd.DataFrame(
   ....:     {"mean": mean, "std": 0.5 * mean, "mu": mu_s.to_series("flux"), "sigma": sigma_s.to_series("flux")}
   ....: ).iloc[[0, 5, 9, 12]].round(3)
   ....: 
Out[32]: 
       mean    std     mu  sigma
cell                            
0     0.705  0.353  0.503  0.497
5     0.017  0.009 -1.734  0.242
9     0.172  0.086 -0.506  0.294
12    2.233  1.116  2.183  1.197

Far above the scale, \(u\)’s prior is the value’s own; near zero it is centered lower and wider. Solving and reading are the same as for the lognormal model:

In [33]: post_s = fips.solve(build(obs, softplus, mu_s, sigma_s), "gauss_newton")

In [34]: softplus.transform(post_s.estimate).to_series("flux").iloc[3:7].round(2)
Out[34]: 
cell
3    0.57
4    1.36
5    0.01

Name: estimate, dtype: float64

What the posterior reports#

The posterior is that of "gauss_newton", a sampled one, in the units of \(u\) (A nonlinear forward model, “What the posterior reports”; the members table). For the value:

  • model.transform(post.estimate) is the value at the mode, near each element’s posterior median. The mean is higher: for the lognormal model, by about \(e^{\hat\sigma^2/2}\), with \(\hat\sigma\) the posterior standard deviation of \(u\).

  • model.transform(post.sample(n)) gives draws of the value. An element’s interval is the quantiles of its draws; an aggregate is the aggregate of each draw, read off the draws.

  • variances, cov() and uncertainty() are those of \(u\), from the kept draws. post.sample(n, W=W) and trials() aggregate \(u\), which is not an aggregate of the value: write the loop over the draws yourself (Reading a transformed state).

The draws are of the posterior itself, not of the Gaussian at the mode, so in the problem’s own world a total’s interval holds the truth about as often as it claims. An element’s own spread is harder for the chain: where the observations see only a sum, compare a few seed values before trusting it (A nonlinear forward model, “Warnings and checks”). The Gaussian at the mode can be far from the posterior for a total of many elements (How it works, below).

Options#

The options are the models’ arguments. The solve’s options (tol, max_iterations, draws, burn_in, seed, gamma) are those of "gauss_newton" (A nonlinear forward model).

Argument

What it is

H

The forward operator on the whole state, an Operator: the transformed block and any other. Its form is kept (sparse stays sparse).

reference (LogNormalForward)

The reference \(r\) of each element of the block, not negative: a Vector on the block, a Series on its labels, or one number for every element.

scale (SoftplusForward, required)

The scale \(s\) of the bend, positive, in the value’s units.

block

The block that is \(u\). Default None: the state’s one block. Every other block passes through as it is.

Each model has two methods:

  • prior_for(mean, std): the prior on \(u\) (\(\mu\) and \(\sigma\), two Vectors on the block) whose value has the mean and the standard deviation given. Each argument is a Vector, a Series or a number. For the lognormal model a mean and a standard deviation are zero exactly where the reference is zero; for the softplus model a mean is positive.

  • transform(x): a state (a Vector, such as post.estimate) or draws (a DataFrame, such as post.sample(n)) with the block as the value, on the same labels.

Warnings and checks#

The models raise when they are built:

  • TypeError if H is not an Operator, or reference is an array (it has no labels);

  • ValueError if the state has several blocks and block is not given, reference does not have exactly the block’s labels or is negative or not finite, or scale is not a positive, finite number;

  • KeyError if block is not a block of the state.

prior_for raises ValueError if a mean or a standard deviation is negative or not finite, or does not have the block’s labels; for the lognormal model, if a mean is zero where the reference is not (a lognormal value is never zero), or not zero where the reference is (a positive value there needs a reference, or the softplus model); for the softplus model, if a mean is not positive. The softplus prior_for raises RuntimeError if it cannot match some element.

transform and the model raise ValueError if the value overflows: a \(u\) that large is far outside any prior on it; check the prior’s units.

The solve warns and raises as "gauss_newton" does (A nonlinear forward model): a wide prior makes the problem more nonlinear, and a solve that has not stopped after max_iterations raises. Raise it, or narrow the prior. fips logs its warnings through Python’s logging, which is silent until you configure it (Repeated inversions, logging and saving).

Cost#

The cost is that of "gauss_newton" (A nonlinear forward model): an analytic solve per step, then the draws. A transform is cheaper than a model written by hand: its Jacobian keeps the form of \(H\) (the sampler never forms it), and its known second derivatives give Newton’s steps where Gauss-Newton’s would crawl. The wider the prior on \(u\), the more the model bends, and the more steps both the solve and each draw take. The softplus prior_for is cheap next to the solve.

How it works#

The models. The observations are \(F(x) = H\, [T(u); \text{the other blocks}] + c\), and the Jacobian is \(H \operatorname{diag}(T'(u), 1)\). The lognormal model has \(T(u) = r\, e^{u}\); the softplus model \(T(u) = s \log(1 + e^{u/s})\), which is \(u\) for \(u \gg s\) and \(s\, e^{u/s}\), near zero and positive, for \(u \ll -s\).

The lognormal prior. A prior standard deviation of \(\sigma\) on \(u\) is a factor of \(e^{\sigma}\) either way. Where the prior on \(u\) is centered sets what the reference is:

  • with \(u \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\) the reference is the prior median of the value, and its mean is \(e^{\sigma^2/2}\) times the reference (1.65 times for \(\sigma = 1\));

  • with \(u \sim N(-\sigma^2/2, \sigma^2)\) the reference is the prior mean of the value.

A reference is usually an estimate of the mean, and a total is a sum of means, so prior_for matches the mean. For a mean \(m\) and standard deviation \(s\) of the value,

\[\sigma^2 = \log\left(1 + \frac{s^2}{m^2}\right), \qquad \mu = \log\frac{m}{r} - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}.\]

Two elements whose \(u\) correlate by \(\rho\) have values that correlate less,

\[\frac{e^{\rho \sigma_i \sigma_j} - 1}{\sqrt{(e^{\sigma_i^2} - 1)(e^{\sigma_j^2} - 1)}},\]

0.38 for \(\rho = 0.5\) and \(\sigma = 1\).

The softplus prior has no closed form. prior_for computes the value’s mean and standard deviation for a Gaussian \(u\) by quadrature, and matches them to the ones asked for by Newton’s method, to \(10^{-12}\). Far above the scale it returns the value’s own prior; near zero, a \(u\) centered lower and wider. Its correlations are those of \(u\), and the value’s are the same far above the scale, weaker near zero.

Why an aggregate comes from the posterior’s draws. The posterior of \(u\) is not Gaussian, and the Gaussian at the mode can be far from it for a function of many elements. Two elements whose sum is observed closely, each with a prior factor of \(e\) either way, show it:

In [35]: two = fips.Space({"flux": pd.Index(["a", "b"], name="cell")})

In [36]: one = fips.Space({"conc": pd.RangeIndex(1, name="obs_id")})

In [37]: pair = fips.LogNormalForward(fips.Operator(np.ones((1, 2)), one, two), 1.0)

In [38]: m, s = pair.prior_for(1.0, np.sqrt(np.e - 1.0))  # sigma = 1

In [39]: sums = fips.Problem(m, fips.Independent().with_std(s), fips.Vector([2.0], one), fips.Independent().with_std(0.05), pair)

In [40]: post2 = fips.solve(sums, "gauss_newton")

In [41]: gaussian = pair.transform(fips.solve(sums.linearized(post2.estimate)).sample(2000, rng=0)).sum()

In [42]: exact = pair.transform(post2.sample(2000, rng=0)).sum()

In [43]: pd.DataFrame({"Gaussian at the mode": gaussian.agg(["mean", "std"]), "posterior": exact.agg(["mean", "std"])}).round(2)
Out[43]: 
      Gaussian at the mode  posterior
mean                  2.57       2.00
std                   0.93       0.05

The data say the sum is \(2.00 \pm 0.05\), and so do the posterior’s draws; the Gaussian at the mode’s do not. The states that give that sum lie on a curve, \(e^{u_a} + e^{u_b} = 2\). The Gaussian at the mode lies along the curve’s tangent, and on the tangent the sum of the exponentials is never below 2. The values at the mode are right (1 and 1); the Gaussian’s draws are not. Any observation of a sum of elements is this case, more so the wider the prior. The sampler uses the Gaussian at the mode only to move, and its draws are of the posterior (A nonlinear forward model).

The solve is "gauss_newton"’s, with Newton’s steps, since the transform’s second derivatives are known (A nonlinear forward model, How it works).
