Fitting the covariance settings#

Every inversion rests on settings someone chose: the prior error’s standard deviation and correlation length, the model-data mismatch’s standard deviation. The rule: the settings that make the observations most probable are their maximum-likelihood estimates, and post.log_evidence is that probability. Write a function that builds the problem from the settings, and maximize the evidence over them with scipy.optimize (Michalak et al., 2005). fips adds no fitting function: the settings are yours, and so is the function that turns them into a problem.

What the evidence is#

With the state integrated out, the observations of a linear problem are Gaussian: \(z \sim N(H x_0 + c, G)\), with \(G = H S_0 H^T + S_z / \gamma\) the covariance of the innovation (innovation_cov). The evidence is the density of the observations under it:

\[\ln p(z) = -\tfrac{1}{2} \left( \chi^2 + \ln \lvert G \rvert + m \ln 2\pi \right),\]

with \(\chi^2 = d^T G^{-1} d\) (chi2) and \(d = z - H x_0 - c\). The two terms pull opposite ways: a wider prior or a larger mismatch lowers \(\chi^2\) and raises \(\ln \lvert G \rvert\), so the evidence is largest at covariances as large as the innovation needs and no larger. fips reads \(\ln \lvert G \rvert\) from what the solve computed (it is \(\ln \lvert S_z / \gamma \rvert\) plus twice the information_content), so the evidence costs one \(\ln \lvert S_z \rvert\) beyond the solve, and post.with_gamma(g) gives another gamma’s for a few products. Gamma scales the mismatch, so it is the same setting as the mismatch’s standard deviation: fit one or the other, not both.

A world at known settings#

A hundred and fifty cells along a line, 1 km apart, with a prior of 2 and errors correlated over a length the fit should find; two hundred observations, each of one to four neighboring cells:

In [1]: import numpy as np

In [2]: import pandas as pd

In [3]: from scipy.optimize import brentq, minimize

In [4]: import fips

In [5]: rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

In [6]: n_cells, n_obs = 150, 200

In [7]: cells = fips.Space({"flux": pd.Index(np.arange(n_cells) + 0.5, name="x")})  # km

In [8]: observed = fips.Space({"ch4": pd.RangeIndex(n_obs, name="obs_id")})

In [9]: footprint = np.zeros((n_obs, n_cells))

In [10]: for i, start in enumerate(rng.integers(0, n_cells - 4, n_obs)):
   ....:     footprint[i, start : start + rng.integers(1, 5)] = rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5)
   ....: 

In [11]: H = fips.Operator(footprint, observed, cells)

In [12]: prior = fips.Vector(np.full(n_cells, 2.0), cells, name="prior")

The function from the settings to the problem is the one a repeated inversion already has (the user guide). Here the settings are three numbers:

In [13]: def build(settings, obs):
   ....:     """The problem at the settings: the prior std, the correlation length (km), the mismatch std."""
   ....:     std, length, noise = settings
   ....:     return fips.Problem(
   ....:         prior=prior,
   ....:         prior_cov=fips.Exponential("x", length).with_std(std),
   ....:         obs=obs,
   ....:         obs_cov=fips.Independent().with_std(noise),
   ....:         forward=H,
   ....:     )
   ....:

The world is the problem at the true settings, and simulate() draws a truth from its prior and the observations it would give:

In [14]: true = pd.Series({"std": 1.0, "length": 5.0, "noise": 0.5})

In [15]: world = build(true, fips.Vector(np.zeros(n_obs), observed))

In [16]: truth, obs = world.simulate(rng)

Maximizing the evidence#

Minimize the negative log evidence over the logarithms of the settings, which keeps each positive and puts a factor of two the same distance away on either side. L-BFGS-B estimates the gradient from differences, at a few solves each:

In [17]: def cost(log_settings, obs=obs):
   ....:     return -fips.solve(build(np.exp(log_settings), obs)).log_evidence
   ....: 

In [18]: guess = pd.Series({"std": 0.3, "length": 1.0, "noise": 2.0})

In [19]: fit = minimize(cost, np.log(guess), method="L-BFGS-B")

In [20]: fitted = pd.Series(np.exp(fit.x), index=true.index)

In [21]: pd.DataFrame({"true": true, "guess": guess, "fitted": fitted}).round(3)
Out[21]: 
        true  guess  fitted
std      1.0    0.3   0.977
length   5.0    1.0   2.839
noise    0.5    2.0   0.475

From a guess off by a factor of three to five in each setting, the fit finds the two standard deviations to 2% and 5% of the truth, and a correlation length of 2.8 km against the true 5. That is not a failure of the fit: one map of 150 cells holds about thirty correlation lengths, and the evidence says little more about the length than that. Hold the length at each of several values and fit the two standard deviations (a profile): the evidence changes by less than 1.5 between 2 and 5 km, and a drop of 2 below the maximum is roughly the edge of a 95% interval (half of 3.84, the 95% point of a chi-squared with one degree of freedom):

In [22]: def profile(length):
   ....:     best = minimize(lambda s: cost(np.log([np.exp(s[0]), length, np.exp(s[1])])), np.log([1.0, 0.5]), method="L-BFGS-B")
   ....:     return pd.Series({"log_evidence": -best.fun, "std": np.exp(best.x[0]), "noise": np.exp(best.x[1])})
   ....: 

In [23]: table = pd.DataFrame({L: profile(L) for L in (1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0, 8.0, 12.0)}).T.rename_axis("length")

In [24]: table["below_max"] = -fit.fun - table["log_evidence"]

In [25]: table.round(3)
Out[25]: 
        log_evidence    std  noise  below_max
length                                       
1.0         -276.411  0.946  0.447      7.912
2.0         -269.326  0.934  0.464      0.827
3.0         -268.517  0.987  0.476      0.018
5.0         -269.976  1.136  0.488      1.477
8.0         -272.282  1.364  0.494      3.783
12.0        -274.228  1.635  0.497      5.729

The data rule out lengths of 1 km and of 8 km and more, and leave 2 to 5 km about equally likely. Over other worlds at the same settings the fits scatter about the truth; their medians, over ten worlds, are within 2% of it:

In [26]: fits = []

In [27]: for trial in range(10):
   ....:     _, obs_k = world.simulate(rng)
   ....:     best = minimize(cost, np.log(guess), args=(obs_k,), method="L-BFGS-B")
   ....:     fits.append(np.exp(best.x))
   ....: 

In [28]: fits = pd.DataFrame(fits, columns=true.index)

In [29]: fits.median().round(3)
Out[29]: 
std       1.006
length    5.064
noise     0.507
dtype: float64

The scatter of the fits is the uncertainty of the settings, which the posterior at the fitted settings leaves out: it treats them as known. To carry it into the flux, the settings need a prior of their own and draws over them: The uncertainty of the settings draws them.

Against a reduced chi-squared of 1#

The common alternative is to sweep a setting until the reduced chi-squared, \(\chi^2 / m\), is 1 (reduced_chi2). It is one equation, so it picks one setting given the others: each guess of the length and the mismatch has a prior std that passes it. Here are four, each with its evidence:

In [30]: def chi2_of_one(length, noise):
   ....:     def excess(log_std):
   ....:         return fips.solve(build([np.exp(log_std), length, noise], obs)).reduced_chi2 - 1.0
   ....:     std = np.exp(brentq(excess, np.log(0.01), np.log(100.0)))
   ....:     evidence = fips.solve(build([std, length, noise], obs)).log_evidence
   ....:     return pd.Series({"length": length, "noise": noise, "std": std, "log_evidence": evidence})
   ....: 

In [31]: pd.DataFrame([chi2_of_one(L, s) for L, s in ((1.0, 2.0), (2.0, 1.0), (5.0, 0.5), (5.0, 0.3))]).round(3)
Out[31]: 
   length  noise    std  log_evidence
0     1.0    2.0  0.319      -431.390
1     2.0    1.0  0.485      -326.523
2     5.0    0.5  1.099      -270.060
3     5.0    0.3  3.126      -334.078

Every row has a reduced chi-squared of 1, and their prior stds run from 0.3 to 3.1: the test passes whatever the guess, so it cannot say which guess is right. Only the row at the true length and mismatch gets the right prior std (1.1), and its evidence is near the maximum (-268.5); the others are 58 to 163 below it, odds against them of \(e^{58}\) and more. The evidence compares the rows, where the reduced chi-squared cannot.

At the maximum of the evidence the reduced chi-squared is 1 by itself:

In [32]: round(fips.solve(build(fitted, obs)).reduced_chi2, 4)
Out[32]: 1.0

Scaling the prior std and the mismatch std together by \(k\) scales \(G\) by \(k^2\), and the evidence is stationary in \(k\) exactly when \(\chi^2 = m\). So a reduced chi-squared of 1 is a consequence of the maximum, one condition of three, not a way to find it.

The other methods#

"gauss_newton" (A nonlinear forward model, Fluxes that stay positive) gives Laplace’s approximation of the evidence: the same formula with the cost at the mode and the information content of the Gaussian there. For a lognormal model in four cells with a log-std of 0.8, it was within about 0.1 of an importance-sampling estimate, a small error next to the differences between settings above; it is exact for a linear model, and it leaves out the skew of the posterior. Fit lognormal settings with it as above. "bounded" (Bounds on the state) raises: the evidence with bounds needs the probability of the bounds under the prior and under the posterior, which fips does not compute. Fit the settings of a bounded inversion on its analytic problem. "variational" (A problem too large to factor: the variational method) gives the evidence of its low-rank posterior, exact at full rank. Below it the evidence leaves out the information of the pairs not kept and is too high, the more so for a larger prior, so a maximum would pick too large a prior: it raises while the last pair kept still has an eigenvalue above 1, and is exact only when rank reaches the problem’s.
