Fitting the covariance settings#

Every inversion rests on numbers someone chose: the prior covariance’s standard deviation and correlation length, the observation covariance’s standard deviation. These are the settings. The settings that make the observations most probable are their maximum-likelihood estimates, and post.log_evidence is the log of that probability, the evidence. Write a function that builds the problem from the settings, and maximize the evidence over them with scipy.optimize:

def cost(log_settings):
    return -fips.solve(build(np.exp(log_settings))).log_evidence

fit = scipy.optimize.minimize(cost, np.log(guess), method="L-BFGS-B")

np.exp(fit.x) is the fitted settings, and fips.solve(build(...)) at them is the posterior. fips has no fitting function of its own: the settings are yours, and so is the function that turns them into a problem (Michalak et al., 2005).

When to use it#

  • Your settings are a judgment, and you want the data’s view of them. Fit the ones you are least sure of; hold the others.

  • The reduced chi-squared is far from 1. It says the covariances are too small or too large together, not which one is wrong; the evidence decides between them (Against a reduced chi-squared of 1).

  • Only gamma, one factor on the observation covariance. post.with_gamma(g).log_evidence gives the evidence at another gamma for a few products, with no new solve, so a sweep or scipy.optimize.minimize_scalar() over gamma is cheap. Gamma scales the observation covariance, so it is the same setting as the observation standard deviation: fit one or the other, not both.

  • The fitted settings are uncertain, and that matters to the answer. A posterior at fitted settings treats them as known. The uncertainty of the settings draws the settings from their posterior instead, and carries their uncertainty into the state’s.

An example#

A quantity along a line: 150 state elements one unit apart, with a prior of 2, and 200 observations, each a weighted sum of one to four neighboring elements.

In [1]: import numpy as np

In [2]: import pandas as pd

In [3]: from scipy.optimize import brentq, minimize

In [4]: import fips

In [5]: rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

In [6]: n, m = 150, 200

In [7]: line = fips.Space({"field": pd.Index(np.arange(n) + 0.5, name="x")})

In [8]: observed = fips.Space({"obs": pd.RangeIndex(m, name="obs_id")})

In [9]: weights = np.zeros((m, n))

In [10]: for i, start in enumerate(rng.integers(0, n - 4, m)):
   ....:     weights[i, start : start + rng.integers(1, 5)] = rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5)
   ....: 

In [11]: H = fips.Operator(weights, observed, line)

In [12]: prior = fips.Vector(np.full(n, 2.0), line, name="prior")

The function from the settings to the problem is the one a repeated inversion already has. Here the settings are three numbers: the prior standard deviation, the correlation length, and the observation standard deviation.

In [13]: def build(settings, obs):
   ....:     """Return the problem at the settings (prior std, correlation length, observation std)."""
   ....:     std, length, noise = settings
   ....:     return fips.Problem(
   ....:         prior=prior,
   ....:         prior_cov=fips.Exponential("x", length).with_std(std),
   ....:         obs=obs,
   ....:         obs_cov=fips.Independent().with_std(noise),
   ....:         forward=H,
   ....:     )
   ....:

To show that the fit finds the right settings, the observations here come from a world whose settings are known: the problem at the true settings, whose simulate() draws a truth from its prior and the observations it gives (Synthetic experiments).

In [14]: true = pd.Series({"std": 1.0, "length": 5.0, "noise": 0.5})

In [15]: truth, obs = build(true, fips.Vector(np.zeros(m), observed)).simulate(rng)

Minimize the negative log evidence over the logarithms of the settings. The logarithm keeps each setting positive and puts a factor of two the same distance away on either side. L-BFGS-B estimates the gradient from differences, a few solves per step:

In [16]: def cost(log_settings, obs=obs):
   ....:     return -fips.solve(build(np.exp(log_settings), obs)).log_evidence
   ....: 

In [17]: guess = pd.Series({"std": 0.3, "length": 1.0, "noise": 2.0})

In [18]: fit = minimize(cost, np.log(guess), method="L-BFGS-B")

In [19]: fitted = pd.Series(np.exp(fit.x), index=true.index)

In [20]: pd.DataFrame({"true": true, "guess": guess, "fitted": fitted}).round(3)
Out[20]: 
        true  guess  fitted
std      1.0    0.3   0.977
length   5.0    1.0   2.839
noise    0.5    2.0   0.475

From a guess off by a factor of three to five in each setting, the fit finds both standard deviations close to the truth. The correlation length it finds is shorter than the true one, and the next section shows why that is not a failure of the fit.

How well the data decide a setting#

The evidence is a likelihood, so its curvature says how well the data decide a setting. Hold one setting at each of several values, fit the others, and read how far the evidence falls below its maximum (a profile). A fall of about 2 is the edge of a 95% interval: half of 3.84, the 95% point of a chi-squared with one degree of freedom.

In [21]: def profile(length):
   ....:     """Fit the two standard deviations with the length held, and return the evidence there."""
   ....:     best = minimize(lambda s: cost(np.log([np.exp(s[0]), length, np.exp(s[1])])), np.log([1.0, 0.5]), method="L-BFGS-B")
   ....:     return pd.Series({"log_evidence": -best.fun, "std": np.exp(best.x[0]), "noise": np.exp(best.x[1])})
   ....: 

In [22]: table = pd.DataFrame({L: profile(L) for L in (1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0, 8.0, 12.0)}).T.rename_axis("length")

In [23]: table["below_max"] = -fit.fun - table["log_evidence"]

In [24]: table.round(3)
Out[24]: 
        log_evidence    std  noise  below_max
length                                       
1.0         -276.411  0.946  0.447      7.912
2.0         -269.326  0.934  0.464      0.827
3.0         -268.517  0.987  0.476      0.018
5.0         -269.976  1.136  0.488      1.477
8.0         -272.282  1.364  0.494      3.783
12.0        -274.228  1.635  0.497      5.729

The data rule out the shortest and the longest lengths and leave the middle of the range about equally likely: a line of 150 elements holds only a few dozen correlation lengths, and its observations say little more about the length than that. A flat profile is the data’s answer, not the optimizer’s failure; when it matters to the answer, The uncertainty of the settings carries it into the state’s uncertainty.

Which methods give the evidence#

The evidence is exact for a Gaussian posterior, Laplace’s approximation for a posterior summarized at its mode, and refused where neither holds:

  • "analytic": exact.

  • "gauss_newton" (A nonlinear forward model, Values that stay positive): Laplace’s approximation, the same formula with the misfit at the mode and the information content of the Gaussian there. It is exact for a linear forward model. For a model that bends, it leaves out how the posterior departs from that Gaussian (its skew); for a lognormal state of a few elements that error is about 0.1, small next to the differences between settings, so fit the settings of a forward model with it as above.

  • "variational" (A problem too large to factor: the variational method): the same formula with the information content of its low-rank posterior (Laplace’s at the mode, with a forward model). It is exact at full rank. Below it, the evidence leaves out the information of the directions not kept and is too high, the more so for a larger prior, so a maximum would pick too large a prior. It raises while the last direction kept still has an eigenvalue above 1: raise rank until the last eigenvalues are well below 1.

  • "bounded" (Bounds on the state): raises with a finite bound. The evidence with bounds needs the probability of the bounds under the prior and under the posterior, which fips does not compute. Fit the settings on the problem without bounds, with "analytic". With no finite bound it is the analytic posterior, and gives the exact evidence.

  • "ensemble" (A model without a Jacobian: the ensemble method): raises. For a linear problem, the analytic solve of the same problem gives the exact evidence.

A method that cannot give it raises UnavailableError, saying why. The members table lists it with the rest.

Against a reduced chi-squared of 1#

The common alternative is to adjust a setting until the reduced chi-squared, \(\chi^2 / m\) (reduced_chi2), is 1. It is one equation, so it picks one setting given the others: each guess of the length and the observation standard deviation has a prior standard deviation that passes it. Here are four guesses, each with the prior standard deviation that gives a reduced chi-squared of 1, and the evidence there:

In [25]: def chi2_of_one(length, noise):
   ....:     """Return the prior std at which the reduced chi-squared is 1, and the evidence there."""
   ....:     def excess(log_std):
   ....:         return fips.solve(build([np.exp(log_std), length, noise], obs)).reduced_chi2 - 1.0
   ....:     std = np.exp(brentq(excess, np.log(0.01), np.log(100.0)))
   ....:     evidence = fips.solve(build([std, length, noise], obs)).log_evidence
   ....:     return pd.Series({"length": length, "noise": noise, "std": std, "log_evidence": evidence})
   ....: 

In [26]: pd.DataFrame([chi2_of_one(L, s) for L, s in ((1.0, 2.0), (2.0, 1.0), (5.0, 0.5), (5.0, 0.3))]).round(3)
Out[26]: 
   length  noise    std  log_evidence
0     1.0    2.0  0.319      -431.390
1     2.0    1.0  0.485      -326.523
2     5.0    0.5  1.099      -270.060
3     5.0    0.3  3.126      -334.078

Every row passes the test, with prior standard deviations a factor of ten apart, so the test cannot say which guess is right. The evidence can: only the row at the true length and observation standard deviation comes near its maximum, and a difference in log evidence is the log of the odds between two rows. At the maximum of the evidence, the reduced chi-squared is 1 by itself:

In [27]: round(fips.solve(build(fitted, obs)).reduced_chi2, 4)
Out[27]: 1.0

A reduced chi-squared of 1 is one condition the maximum satisfies, not a way to find it (How it works).

Warnings and checks#

  • The evidence is a density of the observations, so it depends on their units and their number. Compare it only between problems of the same observations.

  • A setting the data do not decide has a flat evidence, and the optimizer stops anywhere along the flat part. Check a fitted setting with a profile before trusting it.

  • The observation covariance must be positive definite: the evidence needs \(\ln \lvert S_z \rvert\), and raises numpy.linalg.LinAlgError otherwise. A singular prior covariance is fine.

  • Gamma and the observation standard deviation are one setting. Fitting both leaves the optimizer a direction in which the evidence does not change.

Cost#

Each evaluation is one solve. L-BFGS-B with differences takes one more solve per setting at each step, so a fit of \(p\) settings costs a few dozen times \(p + 1\) solves. The evidence itself costs one \(\ln \lvert S_z \rvert\) beyond the solve, and post.with_gamma(g).log_evidence a few products, after one eigendecomposition that every gamma shares.

How it works#

With the state integrated out, the observations of a linear problem are Gaussian, \(z \sim N(H x_0 + c, G)\), with \(G = H S_0 H^T + S_z / \gamma\) the covariance of the innovation (innovation_cov). The evidence is the density of the observations under it:

\[\ln p(z) = -\tfrac{1}{2} \left( \chi^2 + \ln \lvert G \rvert + m \ln 2\pi \right),\]

with \(\chi^2 = d^T G^{-1} d\) (chi2) and \(d = z - H x_0 - c\). The two terms pull opposite ways: a wider prior or a larger observation error lowers \(\chi^2\) and raises \(\ln \lvert G \rvert\), so the evidence is largest at covariances as large as the innovation needs and no larger.

fips reads \(\ln \lvert G \rvert\) from what the solve computed: \(\lvert G \rvert = \lvert S_z / \gamma \rvert \, \lvert I + \gamma S_z^{-1} H S_0 H^T \rvert\), and the log of the second factor is twice the information_content. So

\[\ln p(z) = -\tfrac{1}{2} \left( \chi^2 + 2 \mathcal{H} + \ln \lvert S_z \rvert - m \ln \gamma + m \ln 2\pi \right),\]

with \(\mathcal{H}\) the information content: only \(\ln \lvert S_z \rvert\) is computed beyond the solve, once per problem. Laplace’s approximation, for a forward model, is the same formula with the misfit at the mode and the information content of the Gaussian there.

Why the reduced chi-squared is 1 at the maximum. Scaling the prior and the observation standard deviations together by \(k\) scales \(G\) by \(k^2\), so the evidence along that direction is \(-\tfrac{1}{2} (\chi^2 / k^2 + m \ln k^2) + \text{const}\). It is stationary in \(k\) exactly when \(\chi^2 = m\). The maximum over all the settings is stationary in that direction too, so its reduced chi-squared is 1; the other directions (how the variance splits between the prior and the observations, the correlation length) the reduced chi-squared does not see.
